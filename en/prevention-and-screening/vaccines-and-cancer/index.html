Prevention and screening

Vaccines that prevent cancer

Two routine vaccines, against hepatitis B and human papillomavirus, prevent cancer. How they work and who should have them.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

Vaccines against infections that cause cancer

There is no vaccine against "cancer" in general, but there are vaccines against two infections that cause a large share of infection-related cancers.

Hepatitis B

Hepatitis B virus can cause chronic liver infection that, over the years, leads to cirrhosis and liver cancer. The vaccine, available since the 1980s, was the first vaccine to prevent a cancer: in Taiwan, vaccinating newborns from 1984 clearly reduced liver cancer in vaccinated children and young people.

The WHO recommends it for all newborns, and it is also offered to adults at risk (healthcare workers, household contacts of infected people and others).

Human papillomavirus (HPV)

Some HPV types cause almost all cervical cancers and part of anal, vulvar, vaginal, penile and oropharyngeal cancers. The vaccine protects against the highest-risk types.

  • Many countries vaccinate girls and boys around 11 to 13, with catch-up programmes at other ages and for people at higher risk. The WHO now accepts a single dose for young people.
  • It is most effective before sexual activity begins, but also benefits people who have already been sexually active.
  • Its safety has been studied extensively, with hundreds of millions of doses given.

A Swedish study of more than a million women showed that those vaccinated before 17 had an 88% lower risk of cervical cancer. In Scotland, no cases of this cancer have been detected among women vaccinated as girls in the initial programme.

Vaccinated women should still take part in screening, because the vaccine does not cover all types of the virus.

Therapeutic vaccines

Different from the above, therapeutic vaccines aim to train the immune system to attack a cancer that already exists. Some, such as personalised mRNA vaccines, are in advanced clinical trials, especially in melanoma, but are not yet part of standard treatment.
