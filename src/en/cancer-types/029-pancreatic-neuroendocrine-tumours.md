## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | 1-2% of pancreatic tumours; rising because they are found by chance |
| **Types** | Non-functioning (most) and functioning: insulinoma, gastrinoma, glucagonoma, VIPoma, somatostatinoma |
| **Inheritance** | MEN1, von Hippel-Lindau, neurofibromatosis type 1, tuberous sclerosis |
| **Treatment** | Surgery; somatostatin analogues, everolimus, sunitinib, lutetium-177, chemotherapy |
| **Outlook** | Much better than adenocarcinoma: often years or decades |

## Types

**Non-functioning** (60-90%): do not produce hormones causing symptoms; found by chance or because of their size.

**Functioning**, by hormone:

| Tumour | Hormone | Symptoms |
|---|---|---|
| **Insulinoma** | Insulin | Low blood sugar (sweating, confusion, fainting) when fasting; almost always benign |
| **Gastrinoma** | Gastrin | Multiple ulcers and diarrhoea (Zollinger-Ellison syndrome) |
| **Glucagonoma** | Glucagon | Diabetes, migrating skin rash, weight loss |
| **VIPoma** | VIP | Massive watery diarrhoea, low potassium |
| **Somatostatinoma** | Somatostatin | Diabetes, gallstones, fatty diarrhoea |

## Diagnosis

CT, MRI, **endoscopic ultrasound** with biopsy, **gallium-68 DOTATATE PET** (or exendin PET for insulinoma), hormone tests and chromogranin A, and Ki-67 for grade. Genetic testing should be considered, especially in young people or with multiple tumours.

## Treatment

- **Surveillance** for well-differentiated non-functioning tumours under 2 cm (ASPEN study).
- **Surgery**: enucleation, distal pancreatectomy or Whipple depending on location.
- Advanced disease: **somatostatin analogues**, **everolimus**, **sunitinib**, **cabozantinib** (CABINET trial), **capecitabine with temozolomide** (very effective in pancreatic NETs), and **lutetium-177 DOTATATE**.
- Control of hormone symptoms: diazoxide for insulinoma, high-dose proton pump inhibitors for gastrinoma.

## Outlook

Five-year survival exceeds 90% for operated localised tumours, and many patients with metastases live more than 10 years. Steve Jobs had a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour, not an adenocarcinoma.
