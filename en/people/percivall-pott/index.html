Percivall Pott

The London surgeon who in 1775 described chimney sweeps' cancer and showed, for the first time, that a cancer could have an avoidable environmental cause.

An observant surgeon

Percivall Pott was one of the most respected surgeons of 18th-century London, at St Bartholomew's Hospital. His name survives in several conditions, such as tuberculosis of the spine (Pott's disease) and a type of ankle fracture.

The chimney sweeps

In 1775 he published brief observations on cancer of the scrotum in chimney sweeps. They were boys who started work at four or five, often naked, climbing narrow chimneys. Soot stayed in the folds of their skin for years. Pott saw that many of them developed, while still young, a cancer that was extremely rare in other people, and blamed the soot.

Why it matters

It was the first time a cancer was linked to a specific environmental or occupational exposure. It implied that cancer could be prevented by avoiding that exposure. Decades later in Denmark, sweeps were advised to bathe daily, and scrotal cancer declined. In the 20th century the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in soot were identified, the same ones found in tobacco smoke.

Pott is considered one of the founders of cancer epidemiology and occupational health.
