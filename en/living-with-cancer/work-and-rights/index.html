Living with cancer

Work and rights

Sick leave, returning to work, disability protection and the "right to be forgotten" for cancer survivors: what to know.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

Time off work

During treatment many people need sick leave. How long it lasts and how it is paid depend on each country's rules and the employment contract. Not everyone needs to be off the whole time: it depends on the job and the treatment.

Returning to work

Going back to work helps restore normality, but tiredness or brain fog can last months. Some options:

  • Talk to occupational health to adapt the job or hours.
  • Agree a phased return with your employer.
  • Reduced hours, home working or a change of duties, where possible.

In many countries people with cancer are protected as disabled from the moment of diagnosis: in the UK under the Equality Act 2010, and in the US under the Americans with Disabilities Act. Employers must make reasonable adjustments and must not discriminate.

Carers

Many countries offer leave or benefits for people caring for a family member with cancer, including parents of children with cancer. Check what applies where you live.

The right to be forgotten

Several European countries, including France, Belgium, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Portugal, Romania, Italy and Spain, have laws giving cancer survivors a "right to be forgotten": after a set period without relapse from the end of treatment (often five to ten years), they do not have to declare their cancer when taking out insurance or a loan, and companies cannot use it to refuse cover or charge more. The European Union has encouraged all member states to adopt it.

Where to get advice

Hospital social workers and cancer charities give free advice on leave, benefits, discrimination and resources. Rules change: check current details with official sources or a professional.
