## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | 820,000 cases a year worldwide; 3 times more common in women |
| **Types** | Papillary (85%), follicular (5-10%), medullary (2-3%), anaplastic (< 2%) |
| **Risk factors** | Childhood radiation, family history, inherited syndromes |
| **Sign** | A lump in the neck; hoarseness or difficulty swallowing in advanced cases |
| **Treatment** | Active surveillance of microcarcinomas, lobectomy or total thyroidectomy, radioactive iodine |
| **Survival** | Over 98% at five years for papillary |

## What it is

The **thyroid** is a butterfly-shaped gland at the front of the neck that makes hormones regulating metabolism. **Thyroid nodules** are very common (over half of adults have them on ultrasound) and over 90% are benign.

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Papillary** | The most common; slow-growing; may spread to neck nodes, rarely distant spread; *BRAF* V600E mutations or *RET* fusions |
| **Follicular** | May spread through the blood (lung, bone); *RAS* mutations |
| **Oncocytic** (Hürthle cell) | A variant, takes up iodine less well |
| **Medullary** | From the calcitonin-making C cells; 25% inherited; see [its sheet](../medullary-thyroid-cancer/) |
| **Anaplastic** | Very aggressive; see [its sheet](../anaplastic-thyroid-cancer/) |

## Overdiagnosis

Thyroid cancer incidence soared in many countries (South Korea saw a 15-fold rise) through widespread **ultrasound**, which finds **papillary microcarcinomas** (< 1 cm) that mostly would never have caused harm, while deaths did not change. That is why **screening is not recommended** for people without symptoms or risk factors, nor biopsy of small nodules without suspicious ultrasound features.

## Risk factors

- Childhood **radiation** (neck radiotherapy, the **Chernobyl** accident).
- Family history; inherited syndromes (MEN2 for medullary, familial adenomatous polyposis, Cowden, Carney complex).
- Female sex; obesity.

## Diagnosis

1. **Ultrasound** with risk classification (TI-RADS).
2. **Fine needle aspiration (FNA)** of suspicious nodules, reported with the **Bethesda** system.
3. Molecular testing for indeterminate cytology.
4. Thyroid hormone tests; calcitonin if medullary is suspected.
5. Ultrasound of neck nodes.

## Treatment

- **Low-risk papillary microcarcinoma**: **active surveillance** with ultrasound (Japanese experience: under 5% grow significantly in 10 years) or **lobectomy**.
- **Low-risk tumours of 1-4 cm**: **hemithyroidectomy** (lobectomy) or total thyroidectomy.
- **Larger tumours or with nodes**: **total thyroidectomy** with neck dissection.
- **Radioactive iodine (I-131)** after surgery for intermediate-high risk, to destroy remaining tissue and metastases. In low risk it gives no benefit (ESTIMABL2 and HiLo trials).
- Lifelong **levothyroxine** after total thyroidectomy.
- **Iodine-refractory**: **lenvatinib**, sorafenib, cabozantinib; targeted therapy by alteration (**selpercatinib** for *RET*, dabrafenib with trametinib for *BRAF*, larotrectinib for *NTRK*).

## Effects and follow-up

- Surgical risks: injury to the **recurrent laryngeal nerve** (hoarseness) and **parathyroids** (low calcium, tingling).
- Follow-up with blood **thyroglobulin** and ultrasound.
- Quality of life is good, although many patients report tiredness and anxiety around check-ups.

## Outlook

Excellent for papillary and follicular: over 98% at five years and 95% at 20 years. The outlook is worse over 55 with extensive tumours.
