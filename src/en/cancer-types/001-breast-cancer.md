## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | 2.3 million new cases worldwide in 2022 |
| **Who** | Mostly women over 50; about 1% of cases are in men |
| **Typical sign** | A new lump in the breast or armpit, skin or nipple changes |
| **Screening** | Mammography every 2-3 years from about 50 (from 40 in some countries) |
| **Treatment** | Surgery, radiotherapy and systemic treatment according to subtype |
| **Survival** | About 85-90% at five years in high-income countries; over 98% if found while localised |

## What it is

Breast cancer starts in the cells lining the **ducts** (which carry milk to the nipple) or the **lobules** (which make it). When these cells build up changes in their DNA, they multiply out of control. If they have not yet broken through the layer where they started, it is called **carcinoma in situ**; if they have, it is **invasive carcinoma**, which can reach the lymph nodes in the armpit and, through the blood, other organs such as bone, liver, lung or brain.

It is not a single disease. Its behaviour depends mainly on three proteins tested on the biopsy: the **oestrogen and progesterone receptors** and the **HER2** protein, plus the proliferation index **Ki-67**.

## Types and subtypes

By how it looks under the microscope:

- **Invasive carcinoma of no special type** (formerly ductal): 70-80%.
- **Invasive lobular carcinoma**: about 10-15%. It sometimes grows in single files, is harder to see on mammograms and more often affects both breasts.
- Rarer special types: tubular, mucinous, medullary, metaplastic, papillary, and **inflammatory breast cancer**, which makes the breast red, hot and swollen.

By biology, tumours are grouped into **subtypes** that guide treatment:

| Subtype | Features | Approximate share |
|---|---|---|
| **Luminal A** | Hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative, low proliferation | 40-50% |
| **Luminal B** | Hormone receptor-positive, high proliferation or HER2-positive | 15-20% |
| **HER2-positive** | Excess HER2, with or without hormone receptors | 15-20% |
| **Triple-negative** | No hormone receptors or HER2 | 10-15% |

Since 2022 doctors also talk about **HER2-low** tumours, which have some HER2 without being positive, because they respond to some antibody-drug conjugates.

## How common it is

According to GLOBOCAN 2022, breast cancer is the most diagnosed cancer in women in almost every country, with about 2.3 million cases and 670,000 deaths a year worldwide. About one in eight women in high-income countries will develop it during their lifetime. Death rates have been falling for decades thanks to screening and better treatment.

## Risk factors

**Not modifiable**

- **Age**: risk rises with age.
- **Family history** and **inherited variants** in *BRCA1*, *BRCA2*, *PALB2*, *CHEK2*, *ATM*, *TP53* and other genes, responsible for 5-10% of cases.
- **Reproductive and hormonal history**: early first period, late menopause, no children or having them late.
- **Dense breasts** on mammography.
- Radiotherapy to the chest at a young age (for example for lymphoma).
- Earlier lesions such as atypical hyperplasia or lobular carcinoma in situ.

**Modifiable**

- **Alcohol**: risk rises even at low levels.
- **Obesity** after menopause and physical inactivity.
- Combined **hormone replacement therapy** taken for years.
- **Breastfeeding** slightly reduces risk.

## Prevention and screening

**Mammography screening** reduces breast cancer deaths in women who take part. Most European programmes invite women aged about **50 to 69-74** every two years; the UK NHS screens every three years from 50 to 71, and US guidance recommends screening every two years from 40. Like any screening, it has limits: false positives, missed cancers and overdiagnosis.

Women at **high risk** (carriers of *BRCA* or other gene variants, strong family history or earlier chest radiotherapy) follow their own programme, with yearly **MRI** from a younger age, and may consider **risk-reducing surgery** or preventive drugs such as tamoxifen.

Breast self-examination has not been shown to reduce deaths as a programme, but knowing your breasts helps you seek advice about a change.

## Symptoms

- A new **lump** in the breast or armpit, usually painless.
- Changes in breast size or shape.
- **Orange-peel skin**, dimpling or redness.
- A **nipple** that newly turns in, has bloody discharge or a sore that does not heal.

Most breast lumps are benign (cysts, fibroadenomas), but any persistent change should be checked.

## Diagnosis

1. **Examination** and **mammography**, often with **ultrasound**. Findings are classified using the BI-RADS system.
2. Image-guided **core needle biopsy**, which confirms the diagnosis and allows testing of receptors, HER2 and Ki-67.
3. **MRI** in selected cases (lobular cancer, dense breasts, high risk, assessing response to chemotherapy).
4. **Staging tests** (CT, bone scan or PET-CT) only for large tumours, affected nodes or symptoms.
5. **Genetic testing** when criteria are met: family history, young age, triple-negative cancer, cancer in a man or tumours in both breasts.

In hormone receptor-positive tumours with no or few affected nodes, **genomic tests** (Oncotype DX, MammaPrint and others) help decide whether chemotherapy adds benefit.

## Stages

The TNM system is used; the 8th AJCC edition adds grade, receptors and HER2 to form the **prognostic stage**:

- **Stage 0**: carcinoma in situ.
- **Stages I and II**: tumour confined to the breast, with no or few affected nodes.
- **Stage III**: large tumour, many nodes or involvement of the skin or chest wall (locally advanced).
- **Stage IV**: distant metastases.

## Treatment

The plan is decided by a **multidisciplinary team** according to stage, subtype, age and the patient's preferences.

**Early disease (stages I to III)**

- **Surgery**: **breast-conserving surgery** (lumpectomy) followed by radiotherapy is as effective as mastectomy in most cases. **Sentinel lymph node biopsy** avoids clearing the armpit when it is not affected. After mastectomy, immediate or delayed **reconstruction** can be offered.
- **Radiotherapy**: after breast-conserving surgery and in some cases after mastectomy. Short schedules of one to three weeks are now used.
- **Hormone therapy** for receptor-positive tumours, for 5 to 10 years: tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors, sometimes with ovarian suppression. **CDK4/6 inhibitors** (abemaciclib, ribociclib) are added for high-risk tumours.
- **Chemotherapy** depending on risk, often **before** surgery (neoadjuvant) for HER2-positive and triple-negative tumours, because it shows the response and allows later treatment to be adapted.
- **Anti-HER2 drugs** (trastuzumab, pertuzumab, trastuzumab emtansine) for HER2-positive tumours.
- **Immunotherapy** with pembrolizumab for high-risk triple-negative cancer.
- **Olaparib** for *BRCA* carriers at high risk of relapse.

**Metastatic disease (stage IV)**

The aim is to control the cancer for as long as possible with good quality of life. Many women live for years with treatments given in sequence: hormone therapy with CDK4/6 inhibitors first for hormone-sensitive tumours; anti-HER2 drugs and antibody-drug conjugates such as **trastuzumab deruxtecan** for HER2-positive and HER2-low disease; chemotherapy, immunotherapy and **sacituzumab govitecan** for triple-negative disease; and targeted drugs according to mutations (*PIK3CA*, *ESR1*, *BRCA*).

## Outlook

Five-year survival in high-income countries is about **85-90%**. It depends mainly on stage: over 98% for localised tumours and around 30% when there are metastases, a figure that improves with each new treatment. Hormone receptor-positive tumours can relapse late, even after more than ten years, which is why hormone therapy is long.

## After treatment

- Regular **check-ups** with examination and yearly mammography. Routine marker tests or scans are not recommended without symptoms.
- Attention to late effects: arm **lymphoedema**, bone health with aromatase inhibitors, heart health with some treatments, early menopause, sexuality and body image.
- Regular **exercise** and avoiding alcohol and weight gain are linked to fewer relapses.
- **Fertility** should be discussed before treatment starts in young women.

## What's new

Antibody-drug conjugates, immunotherapy for triple-negative disease, CDK4/6 inhibitors in early disease, new oral drugs against the oestrogen receptor (selective degraders) and **liquid biopsy** to detect resistance mutations are the areas changing practice most. **De-escalation** is also being studied: giving less treatment to those who do not need it.

## Questions for your team

- What subtype do I have and what does it mean for my treatment?
- Should I have chemotherapy before surgery?
- Can I keep my breast? Do the lymph nodes in my armpit need removing?
- Should I have genetic testing?
- What side effects should I expect and how are they prevented?
- How will treatment affect my fertility?
