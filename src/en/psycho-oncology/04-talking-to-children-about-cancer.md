## Why it is worth telling them

Children, even very young ones, notice changes: hospital visits, tiredness, crying, whispered conversations. If nobody explains anything, they fill the gaps with their imagination, which is often worse than reality, and may think it is their fault or that they cannot ask. Telling them the truth, adapted to their age, protects their trust and allows them to receive support.

## How to tell them

- Choose a **calm moment** and, if possible, tell them together (both parents, or the parent and a trusted adult).
- Use the real words: **"cancer"**, "tumour", "chemotherapy". Euphemisms cause confusion.
- Explain three key things: **it is not their fault**, **it is not contagious** and **they will be looked after** whatever happens.
- Tell them what will change in their daily life: who will pick them up from school, when you will be in hospital.
- Let them ask, and it is fine to say "I don't know, but I'll tell you when I find out".
- It is normal for them to react with indifference and ask questions days later. Come back to the subject.
- Tell their school, so they can understand changes in behaviour.

## By age

| Age | What they understand | How to help |
|---|---|---|
| **Under 3** | Separations and changes in routine | Keep routines, familiar carers, physical affection |
| **3 to 6** | Simple explanations; may believe they caused it ("magical thinking") | Short, concrete explanations; repeat that it is not their fault; drawings and stories |
| **7 to 12** | More detail; interest in how the body works | Explain the illness and treatment with simple images; involve them in small tasks |
| **Adolescents** | Almost like an adult; may worry about the future and about heredity | Honest information, respect their space and their friends, avoid making them the main carer |

## Difficult questions

- **"Are you going to die?"** Do not promise what you do not know. You can say, for example: "This is a serious illness, the doctors are doing everything they can and we hope the treatment will work. If anything changes, I'll tell you."
- **"Can I catch it?"** No, cancer is not contagious.
- **"Will I get it too?"** Most cancers are not inherited; if there is a hereditary syndrome, it will be explained at the right time.
- If the illness advances, preparing them in advance helps grieving. See [End of life, grief and family](../end-of-life-grief-and-family/).

## Signs that they need help

- Lasting changes in sleep, eating or school performance.
- Going back to behaviours of younger ages (bedwetting, baby talk).
- Isolation, intense irritability, aggressiveness.
- In adolescents: risky behaviours, use of alcohol or drugs, comments about not wanting to live.

Ask your paediatrician, the school or the hospital's psycho-oncology unit. Many cancer charities offer support for children of patients.
