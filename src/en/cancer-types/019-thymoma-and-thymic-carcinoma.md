## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | Very rare: 1-3 cases per million people a year |
| **Who** | Adults aged 40-60 |
| **Association** | **Myasthenia gravis** in a third of thymomas |
| **Treatment** | Complete surgery (thymectomy); radiotherapy and chemotherapy as needed |

## What they are

The **thymus** is an organ in the front of the chest that helps T lymphocytes mature in childhood and then shrinks. Its epithelial tumours are classified by the WHO:

- **Thymomas** (types A, AB, B1, B2 and B3): slow-growing, although they can invade neighbouring structures and the pleura.
- **Thymic carcinoma**: more aggressive, with more frequent spread.
- **Thymic neuroendocrine tumours**: rare, sometimes linked to MEN1.

## Symptoms

A third are found by chance on an X-ray. Others cause cough, chest pain or breathlessness. Thymomas are linked to **autoimmune diseases**: **myasthenia gravis** (muscle weakness, drooping eyelids, double vision), pure red cell aplasia or low antibodies (hypogammaglobulinaemia).

## Diagnosis and treatment

- CT or MRI of the chest; if the appearance is typical and operable, surgery is often done without a prior biopsy.
- **Complete thymectomy**, through the breastbone or by minimally invasive surgery. It is the most important prognostic factor.
- **Radiotherapy** after surgery for invasive tumours or incomplete removal.
- **Chemotherapy** (cisplatin, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, or carboplatin and paclitaxel) for advanced tumours, sometimes before surgery. For recurrent thymic carcinoma, lenvatinib or sunitinib. Immunotherapy is used with caution because of the risk of severe autoimmune reactions.
- Myasthenia should be controlled with a neurologist before anaesthesia.

## Outlook

Completely removed thymomas have five-year survival above 90%; thymic carcinoma, 50-60%. Relapses can appear many years later, so follow-up is long.
