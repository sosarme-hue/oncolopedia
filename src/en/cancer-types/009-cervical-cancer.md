## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | About 660,000 cases and 350,000 deaths a year worldwide |
| **Cause** | Persistent infection with high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV) |
| **Prevention** | HPV vaccination and screening with HPV testing or cytology |
| **Symptoms** | Bleeding after sex, between periods or after menopause; abnormal discharge |
| **Treatment** | Surgery in early stages; chemoradiotherapy with brachytherapy for locally advanced disease |
| **Survival** | About 65-70% at five years; over 90% in early stages |

## What it is

The cervix is the lower part of the womb that opens into the vagina. Most cervical cancers are **squamous cell carcinomas** (70-80%), and the rest **adenocarcinomas**, which start in the glands of the canal and are harder to detect with cytology.

Cancer is preceded for years by **precancerous changes**, called low- or high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (formerly CIN 1, 2 and 3). Finding and treating them prevents cancer.

## The cause: HPV

Almost all cervical cancers are due to **persistent infection** with **high-risk HPV** types, especially 16 and 18, which cause about 70%. HPV is spread by sexual contact and is so common that most sexually active people will have it at some point. In nine out of ten cases the immune system clears it within one or two years. Only when it persists for many years can it cause precancer and, eventually, cancer.

Other factors favouring persistence or progression: **smoking**, immune suppression (HIV, transplants), many pregnancies and long-term use of the contraceptive pill.

Since 2023 it has been recognised that a small share of adenocarcinomas are not caused by HPV, and have a worse outlook.

## Prevention

**HPV vaccination**: many countries vaccinate girls and boys around 11 to 13, with catch-up at other ages. It protects against the types causing most cancers. In Sweden, women vaccinated before 17 had 88% less cervical cancer, and in Scotland no cases have been recorded in women vaccinated as girls in the initial programme.

**Screening**: programmes typically invite women and people with a cervix aged about **25 to 64-65**, using **HPV testing** every five years, with cytology on positive samples. HPV testing is more sensitive than cytology, and some programmes offer **self-sampling** to improve uptake.

The WHO aims to **eliminate** this cancer as a public health problem with its 90-70-90 targets for 2030.

## Symptoms

Precancer and early cancer cause no symptoms. Later there may be:

- **Bleeding** after sex, between periods or after menopause.
- Abnormal vaginal discharge, sometimes smelly.
- Pain during sex.
- In advanced stages: pelvic or back pain, swollen legs, urinary or bowel problems.

## Diagnosis

1. Abnormal **HPV test** or cytology.
2. **Colposcopy**: the cervix is examined with a magnifying lens and **biopsies** are taken from suspicious areas.
3. If there is cancer, pelvic **MRI** to measure the tumour and its extent, and **PET-CT** for locally advanced stages to assess nodes.

## Treating precancer

High-grade changes are treated with a **cone biopsy or LLETZ** (removing a small cone of the cervix, usually with a diathermy loop) under local anaesthetic. Low-grade changes are usually monitored, as many go away on their own.

## Stages (FIGO 2018) and treatment

| Stage | Description | Usual treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **IA** | Microscopic, visible only under the microscope | Cone biopsy or simple hysterectomy |
| **IB1-IB2** | Confined to the cervix, up to 4 cm | **Radical hysterectomy** with lymph node assessment, or **trachelectomy** to preserve fertility in small tumours |
| **IB3-IVA** | Tumour over 4 cm or spread within the pelvis or to nodes | **Chemoradiotherapy** with weekly cisplatin followed by **brachytherapy** |
| **IVB** | Distant spread | Platinum chemotherapy, bevacizumab and **immunotherapy** (pembrolizumab) |

Recent developments:

- The LACC trial showed that **open** radical surgery is safer than **minimally invasive** surgery in this cancer, which changed practice.
- The SHAPE trial showed that for small low-risk tumours a simple hysterectomy is enough.
- Adding **pembrolizumab** to chemoradiotherapy for stage III-IVA (KEYNOTE-A18) improved survival, as did giving induction chemotherapy before chemoradiotherapy (INTERLACE).
- The antibody-drug conjugate **tisotumab vedotin** is used for relapses.

## Outlook

It depends on stage: over 90% five-year survival in stages IA-IB1, about 60-70% for locally advanced disease and under 20% with distant spread. Overall survival is about 65-70%.

## After treatment

Check-ups every few months in the first years. Pelvic radiotherapy can cause early menopause, vaginal dryness and narrowing (dilators are used), bowel or urinary changes and leg lymphoedema. There are treatments for all of these and they are worth discussing.

## Questions for your team

- Can I preserve my fertility?
- Do you recommend surgery or chemoradiotherapy?
- How will treatment affect my sex life and what can I do?
- Would immunotherapy benefit me?
