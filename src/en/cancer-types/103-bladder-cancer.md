| **Main sign** | **Blood in the urine**, even once and without pain |
| **Diagnosis** | Cystoscopy and transurethral resection (TURBT) |
| **Treatment** | TURBT and BCG instillations if it does not invade the muscle; cystectomy or bladder preservation with chemoradiotherapy if it does; enfortumab vedotin with pembrolizumab if it has spread |

## What it is

The **bladder** is lined by the **urothelium**, the same tissue that lines the renal pelvis, ureters and urethra. About 90% of bladder cancers are **urothelial carcinomas**. What changes treatment and outlook most is whether the tumour **invades the muscle layer**:

- **Non-muscle-invasive** (about 75%): confined to the lining (stages Ta, T1 and carcinoma *in situ*). It often recurs but is rarely fatal with good follow-up.
- **Muscle-invasive** (about 25%): grows through the muscle and can spread.

Rarer types include squamous cell carcinoma (linked to schistosomiasis in Africa and the Middle East), adenocarcinoma and small cell carcinoma. Urothelial tumours of the renal pelvis and ureter have [their own page](../upper-tract-urothelial-cancer/). To compare with the kidney, see [Bladder and kidney cancer](../bladder-and-kidney-cancer/).

## How common it is

Spain has one of the highest rates in the world, because of heavy smoking in past decades. It is three to four times more common in men and usually appears after 60.

## Risk factors

- **Smoking**: causes about half of cases and triples or quadruples the risk. Stopping lowers it over the years.
- Workplace exposure to **aromatic amines** and hydrocarbons: dyes, rubber, leather, paints, aluminium. It can be an occupational disease.
- Cyclophosphamide, high-dose pioglitazone, previous pelvic radiotherapy, arsenic in drinking water.
- Chronic **schistosomiasis**.
- Lynch syndrome (mostly in upper tract tumours).

## Symptoms

- **Haematuria**: red, pink or cola-coloured urine, usually **painless**. It may happen once and go away; even so, see a doctor soon.
- Burning, urgency or frequent urination without an infection to explain it, especially with carcinoma *in situ*.
- In advanced disease: pelvic or back pain, swollen legs, weight loss.

## Diagnosis

1. Urine tests and **urine cytology**.
2. **Ultrasound** and **CT urography**, to look at the bladder and rule out tumours in the kidney and ureters.
3. **Cystoscopy**: a thin camera passed through the urethra.
4. **Transurethral resection (TURBT)**: removes the tumour through the urethra and shows whether it invades the muscle.
5. Chest and abdominal CT if it invades the muscle.

## Treatment

**Non-muscle-invasive**:
- Complete **TURBT** and, in many cases, a single chemotherapy instillation into the bladder straight afterwards.
- **BCG instillations** (the tuberculosis vaccine) into the bladder for one to three years at intermediate and high risk: they reduce recurrence and progression.
- **Regular cystoscopies** for life, at gradually longer intervals.
- If BCG does not work: cystectomy or, in selected patients, newer bladder therapies or pembrolizumab.

**Muscle-invasive**:
- Cisplatin **chemotherapy** before surgery; the NIAGARA trial added **durvalumab** before and after.
- **Radical cystectomy** with urinary reconstruction: a **urostomy** (bag on the abdomen) or a **neobladder** made from bowel.
- **Bladder preservation** in selected patients: as complete a TURBT as possible followed by **chemoradiotherapy** (trimodal therapy), with close follow-up.
- **Nivolumab** after surgery if high-risk tumour remains (CheckMate 274 trial).

**Metastatic**:
- **Enfortumab vedotin with pembrolizumab** first line: in the EV-302 trial it almost doubled median survival compared with platinum chemotherapy in previously untreated patients.
- Platinum chemotherapy, with nivolumab or with avelumab maintenance, if the combination above is not possible.
- **Erdafitinib** for *FGFR3* alterations; trastuzumab deruxtecan if HER2 is expressed.

## Outlook

**Non-muscle-invasive** tumours rarely shorten life, but they often recur and need long follow-up; a minority progress and invade the muscle. For **muscle-invasive** tumours, the outlook depends on stage and on the response to chemotherapy before surgery. In metastatic disease, treatments of recent years have clearly lengthened survival, although they rarely cure it.

## After treatment

With a **urostomy**, people learn to change the bag with the help of specialist stoma nurses. With a **neobladder**, they learn to empty it at set times and sometimes with a catheter. Changes in sexual function after cystectomy are common and can be treated. Stopping smoking reduces recurrence.

## Questions for your team

- Does my tumour invade the bladder muscle?
- What is my risk of recurrence and how often will I have cystoscopies?
- Can I keep my bladder?
- If my bladder is removed, which urinary diversion suits me?
- Could my job have played a part, and should I report it?
