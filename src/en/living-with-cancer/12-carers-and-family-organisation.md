## The carer's role

Most care for people with cancer is provided by family and friends: going to appointments, giving medicines, cooking, driving, keeping track of symptoms and, above all, being there. It is a valuable role, and also a tiring one, which often falls on one person, usually a woman. Carers have needs of their own and are entitled to help.

## Organising as a family

- **Choose a main contact** to speak with the medical team and pass on information, so that it does not get distorted.
- **Share out tasks** according to each person's possibilities: appointments, shopping, paperwork, looking after children, keeping company.
- Use a **shared calendar** or a group chat with practical information only.
- Keep a **folder** with reports, the list of medicines, appointments and important phone numbers.
- Respect the patient's **autonomy**: ask what they want to do themselves and what they want to know.
- Accept help from outside: neighbours, friends and volunteers often want to help but do not know how. Give them specific tasks.

## Signs that the carer needs help

- Constant tiredness, insomnia, irritability.
- Having stopped seeing friends or doing anything for yourself.
- Feeling guilty for wanting a rest.
- Sadness, anxiety or physical symptoms that were not there before.

These are signs of **carer burnout**. Talk to your GP and ask about psychological support: many psycho-oncology units also see family members. See [What is psycho-oncology](../../psycho-oncology/what-is-psycho-oncology/).

## Support and leave: the example of Spain

Resources depend on each country. In Spain they include:

| Resource | What it offers |
|---|---|
| **Work leave** | The Workers' Statute provides five days' leave for a relative's serious illness or hospital admission, and reduced hours or unpaid leave to care for a relative. Collective agreements may improve on this |
| **CUME benefit** | For parents who reduce their working hours by at least half to care for a child with cancer or another serious illness |
| **Dependency law** | If the person needs help with basic activities, they can apply for an assessment and access home help, day centres or financial support |
| **Social workers** | Hospital and health centre social workers advise on paperwork and support |
| **Associations** | The Spanish Association Against Cancer (AECC) and others offer free psychological support, volunteers, accommodation and family groups. AECC Infocáncer: **900 100 036**, 24 hours |

In other countries, ask the hospital social worker or national cancer charities (for example, Macmillan Cancer Support in the UK or the American Cancer Society in the US).

## Looking after yourself too

Rest is not selfish: it is what allows you to keep caring. Plan regular breaks, keep up at least one activity of your own, sleep and eat well, and talk about how you feel with someone you trust. If the illness advances, see also [End of life, grief and family](../../psycho-oncology/end-of-life-grief-and-family/).
