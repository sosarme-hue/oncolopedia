Atlas

Oncology atlas

273 malignant entities and subtypes grouped by site, with the reference classification and a plain-language profile of each tumour family.

How to use the atlas. Each subtype is identified by its name in the reference classification (WHO, ICD-O). The text for each family is a general profile, not a description of each subtype: a person's diagnosis, treatment and outlook depend on the pathology report, the stage and their own situation. It cannot be used to choose a treatment.

Breast (10 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Breast imaging and core biopsy. Pathology, grade, estrogen/progesterone receptors and HER2; staging according to risk. Consider genetic counselling based on personal and family history.

How it is treated

Breast-conserving surgery with radiation or mastectomy according to extent. Systemic treatment depends on stage and receptors: endocrine therapy, chemotherapy, anti-HER2 or other targeted agents; immunotherapy in selected settings.

What shapes the outlook

Stage, nodes, grade and hormone/HER2 biology influence risk. Some hormone-sensitive cancers can recur many years later.

Figures

In 2022, female breast cancer accounted for about 11.6% of new cancers worldwide. This groups histologies and does not estimate this subtype.

Lung and pleura (14 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Chest CT, tissue obtained by bronchoscopy, needle biopsy or surgery; histology and immunohistochemistry. Imaging and nodal evaluation as appropriate. Molecular biomarkers and PD-L1 guide options in advanced non-small cell disease.

How it is treated

For localized disease: surgery or stereotactic radiation according to fitness. Locally advanced disease uses combined local and systemic approaches. Advanced disease may receive targeted drugs, immunotherapy and/or chemotherapy depending on subtype, biomarkers and fitness. Small cell cancer and mesothelioma follow distinct pathways.

What shapes the outlook

Extent, resectability, lung function and actionable changes influence outlook. Non-small cell statistics cannot be applied to small cell cancer or mesothelioma.

Figures

Lung: about 2.5 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths worldwide in 2022 (WHO). Not a subtype-specific or pleural cancer estimate.

Digestive system (18 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Endoscopy or organ-specific imaging with histological confirmation. Staging and organ function assessment. Selected biomarkers include MMR/MSI, RAS/BRAF in colorectal cancer and HER2 in specific settings.

How it is treated

Resection or endoscopic treatment may be options for early tumors. Chemotherapy and radiation are integrated according to organ and stage; targeted therapy and immunotherapy require specific indications. GIST and neuroendocrine tumors follow different strategies from adenocarcinoma.

What shapes the outlook

Organ of origin, stage, margins, nodes, grade and molecular biology matter. A single site can contain entities with very different courses.

Figures

Colorectal cancer accounted for about 9.6% and stomach cancer 4.9% of new cancers worldwide in 2022. These are organ categories, not estimates for every digestive tumor.

Liver and biliary tract (8 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Multiphase CT or MRI and liver function assessment. In at-risk patients, hepatocellular cancer may meet validated imaging diagnostic criteria; other settings require biopsy. Biliary cancers need their own evaluation.

How it is treated

Resection, ablation or transplantation for selected hepatocellular cancers; locoregional and systemic therapy according to extent and liver function. Biliary tumors may receive surgery if resectable and systemic therapy; molecular profiling in indicated settings.

What shapes the outlook

Tumor burden, liver function and fitness are considered together. Hepatocellular cancer does not necessarily share cholangiocarcinoma treatment or prognosis.

Figures

Estimates should be consulted separately for liver, gallbladder and biliary tract; no subtype-specific estimate is incorporated here.

Pancreas (5 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Pancreatic-protocol CT and, as appropriate, MRI or endoscopic ultrasound with sampling. CA 19-9 alone does not establish the diagnosis. Distinguish adenocarcinoma from neuroendocrine neoplasms.

How it is treated

Surgery for resectable disease and perioperative systemic treatment according to context. Advanced disease needs systemic treatment and relief of obstruction, pain and malabsorption. Neuroendocrine neoplasms require a different approach.

What shapes the outlook

Resectability and spread are key; histology substantially changes prognosis. Adenocarcinoma statistics do not describe well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors.

Figures

No histology-specific incidence is provided. Consult registries by site and morphology; overall pancreatic figures group different entities.

Gynecological cancers (32 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Examination, imaging and organ-specific biopsy. Endometrial sampling for the uterus; colposcopy/biopsy for the cervix; specialist surgical evaluation for ovarian masses. Molecular classification, MMR and genetic counselling when relevant.

How it is treated

Surgery, radiation and/or systemic therapy according to organ, stage and histology. Endocrine therapy, antiangiogenic drugs, PARP inhibitors or immunotherapy have defined indications and biomarker requirements. Germ cell and stromal tumors have their own management.

What shapes the outlook

Stage, residual disease, histology and molecular classification affect outcome. Do not apply epithelial ovarian cancer statistics to germ cell or uterine tumors.

Figures

Cervix, uterine corpus, ovary, vagina and vulva are separate epidemiological categories. Aggregate figures do not establish the frequency of each histology.

Prostate (3 entities)

How it is diagnosed

PSA interpreted in context, examination, MRI and biopsy when indicated. ISUP/Gleason grade and stage; staging tests according to risk. Elevated PSA alone does not mean cancer.

How it is treated

Active surveillance in selected low-risk disease; surgery or radiation for localized disease according to risk and preferences. Androgen deprivation and other systemic treatments are used in defined settings. Non-acinar subtypes may need another strategy.

What shapes the outlook

PSA, grade, stage and tumor volume help stratify risk. Both indolent and aggressive tumors occur; their outlook should not be conflated.

Figures

Prostate cancer represented about 7.3% of new cancers worldwide in 2022, across both sexes. Organ-level estimate, not subtype-specific.

Kidney and urinary tract (19 entities)

How it is diagnosed

CT/MRI and pathological confirmation as appropriate. Bladder evaluation uses cystoscopy and transurethral resection with muscle sampling to assess invasion. Kidney and urothelial cancers are different diseases.

How it is treated

Kidney: surgery, surveillance or ablation in selected cases; targeted systemic therapy and immunotherapy for specific indications. Urothelial cancer: intravesical treatment, surgery, radiation and/or systemic treatment according to invasion and stage.

What shapes the outlook

Muscle invasion in bladder cancer and stage/biology in kidney cancer are key. Local recurrence risk and risk of spread are not the same.

Figures

Consult registries separately for kidney, renal pelvis, ureter and bladder. This entry provides no specific rate for a rare subtype.

Testis and penis (9 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Testis: ultrasound, AFP, beta-hCG and LDH and urologic evaluation; inguinal orchiectomy commonly establishes diagnosis. Penile cancer needs biopsy and nodal assessment. These pathways are not interchangeable.

How it is treated

Germ cell tumors: surgery and, according to stage and risk, surveillance, platinum-based chemotherapy or radiation in defined settings. Penile cancer: organ-preserving local treatment or surgery and nodal management; systemic therapy in selected indications.

What shapes the outlook

Many germ cell tumors are potentially curable even with spread; risk groups depend on site, markers and metastases. Penile cancer has different prognostic factors.

Figures

Germ cell tumors have a distinctive age distribution. Testicular incidence is not an estimate for penile cancer or for every subtype.

Head and neck (20 entities)

How it is diagnosed

ENT examination, endoscopy, imaging and biopsy. Assess HPV/p16 in oropharyngeal cancer and EBV in nasopharyngeal cancer as indicated. Primary site anatomy guides staging.

How it is treated

Surgery and/or radiation according to site and extent; chemoradiation in defined settings. Systemic treatment for recurrent or metastatic disease. Integrate nutrition, dental care, swallowing and voice rehabilitation.

What shapes the outlook

Site, stage, HPV/EBV status, smoking and fitness matter. Biological differences do not justify de-escalation outside validated protocols.

Figures

Lip/oral, oropharyngeal, laryngeal, nasopharyngeal and salivary cancers should be consulted separately. No histology-specific incidence is included.

Thyroid and endocrine (8 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Ultrasound and cytology/biopsy according to organ; hormone tests as appropriate. Distinguish differentiated, medullary and anaplastic thyroid cancer. For adrenal masses, assess suspected catecholamine secretion before invasive procedures.

How it is treated

Surgery when feasible. Radioiodine is used in selected differentiated thyroid cancer, not medullary or anaplastic disease. Targeted and other treatments depend on molecular changes and extent; adrenal tumors need specialist management.

What shapes the outlook

Histology can radically change the course: papillary cancer outlook cannot be applied to anaplastic cancer. Age, extent and genetic changes matter.

Figures

Thyroid rates depend on diagnostic practices and may be affected by overdiagnosis. No subtype rate is provided, and adrenal cancers are not grouped with thyroid.

Skin (12 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Skin examination and dermoscopy; biopsy for histology. In melanoma: Breslow thickness, ulceration and sentinel node evaluation according to criteria. Other skin cancers use their own staging systems.

How it is treated

Surgery with appropriate margins; radiation and other local options in selected cases. Advanced melanoma: immunotherapy and targeted therapy for appropriate BRAF alterations. Basal cell, squamous cell and Merkel cancers have distinct systemic indications.

What shapes the outlook

Thickness, ulceration, nodes and metastases in melanoma; invasion, immunosuppression and site in other types. There is no single survival estimate for skin cancer.

Figures

Non-melanoma skin cancer registration varies across countries and may be incomplete. Global counts are not directly comparable with melanoma figures.

Brain and nervous system (19 entities)

How it is diagnosed

MRI and an integrated diagnosis combining histology and molecular changes, when tissue can be obtained. Use current CNS entities; not all brain tumors are malignant.

How it is treated

Maximal safe surgery, radiation and/or systemic treatment according to entity, age and location. Some subtypes have specific targets. Rehabilitation, seizure control and neurocognitive support are part of care.

What shapes the outlook

Age, fitness, safe resection and molecular profile affect outlook. Grade and molecular classification are not equivalent to TNM staging in other organs.

Figures

Registries may include nonmalignant CNS tumors; check the behavior included before comparing rates. This catalogue concerns malignant entities.

Eye (5 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Ocular oncology evaluation, eye imaging and staging according to suspicion. Retinoblastoma and uveal melanoma have different diagnostic pathways and biology; assess genetics in retinoblastoma.

How it is treated

Local options aim to preserve the eye and vision when possible: surgery, radiation or other techniques by entity. Retinoblastoma may need systemic or specialized local chemotherapy. Metastatic disease requires a distinct pathway.

What shapes the outlook

Ocular control, vision and metastatic risk are different outcomes. Uveal melanoma should not be managed as cutaneous melanoma.

Figures

These are uncommon cancers; age- and morphology-specific figures are more informative than an aggregate eye cancer total.

Bone (12 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Imaging and a biopsy planned by a sarcoma center to avoid compromising surgery. Histology and molecular testing where relevant; distinguish primary sarcoma from bone metastasis.

How it is treated

Surgery with appropriate margins and entity-specific systemic treatment. Osteosarcoma and Ewing commonly require combination chemotherapy; conventional chondrosarcoma follows a different approach. Radiation in specific indications.

What shapes the outlook

Type, grade, size, site, metastases and response affect prognosis. Resectability and specialist center expertise matter.

Figures

Bone sarcomas are rare and age distributions differ substantially by entity. No subtype-specific rate is supplied.

Soft tissue (21 entities)

How it is diagnosed

MRI or CT according to region and planned core biopsy. Specialist sarcoma pathology review and molecular tests when needed to distinguish entities.

How it is treated

Surgery, radiation and/or systemic treatment tailored to histology and risk. Sarcomas do not all respond to the same chemotherapy; some entities have specific therapeutic targets.

What shapes the outlook

Grade, size, depth, site and metastases are central factors. Natural history can differ even among sarcomas that appear similar.

Figures

Sarcomas comprise many rare entities. Aggregate global data do not estimate incidence for individual histologies.

Leukemias and myeloid neoplasms (17 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Blood count, smear, bone marrow, flow cytometry and cytogenetic/molecular testing. Integrated classification and, when appropriate, measurable residual disease inform management.

How it is treated

Acute leukemias need prompt specialist management by lineage and genetics. Some chronic forms can be observed; others need targeted therapy. Chemotherapy, immunotherapy and transplantation are selected by entity and risk.

What shapes the outlook

Age, genetic changes, comorbidity and response/residual disease matter. TNM and a single survival figure do not apply to all leukemias.

Figures

Incidence and age distribution differ among ALL, AML, CLL, CML and their subtypes. Consult specific registries; no individual rate is provided here.

Lymphomas and plasma cell cancers (34 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Adequate tissue biopsy, preserving architecture when appropriate, immunophenotype and molecular/cytogenetic studies. Staging imaging; marrow and other tests according to entity.

How it is treated

Options range from observation for selected indolent lymphomas to immunochemotherapy for aggressive forms. Radiation, targeted drugs, bispecific antibodies, CAR-T or transplantation depend on entity, line and indication. Myeloma uses its own strategies.

What shapes the outlook

Indolent does not mean harmless, and aggressive does not mean incurable. Entity, prognostic indices, tumor burden and response must be interpreted together.

Figures

Hodgkin lymphoma, non-Hodgkin lymphomas and myeloma are registered separately. Family-level rates do not represent their many subtypes.

Childhood cancers (7 entities)

How it is diagnosed

Pediatric oncology assessment with age- and tumor-specific imaging, pathology and genetics. Consider predisposition syndromes when indicated.

How it is treated

Risk-adapted pediatric protocols combine surgery, chemotherapy, radiation and specific agents. Late-effect surveillance and family support are essential.

What shapes the outlook

Age, stage, histology and biology can change risk group. Adult organ survival data do not apply to pediatric tumors.

Figures

Childhood statistics need age-specific classifications and are often expressed per million. Do not extrapolate adult global figures.
