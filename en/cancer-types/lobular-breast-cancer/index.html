Cancer types

Lobular breast cancer

The second most common type of breast cancer. It grows diffusely, is harder to see on mammograms and behaves in its own way.

At a glance
How common 10-15% of invasive breast cancers
Who Women slightly older than average; linked to hormone replacement therapy
Feature Cells lose the protein E-cadherin and grow loosely, in single files
Diagnosis Can be missed on mammography; MRI helps measure it
Biology Almost always hormone receptor-positive and HER2-negative

What it is

Invasive lobular carcinoma starts in the cells of the lobules, the glands that make milk. Its hallmark is loss of E-cadherin, a protein that holds cells together, through changes in the CDH1 gene. So the cells grow loosely, in lines, without forming a compact mass. This explains why it is sometimes felt not as a lump but as a thickening, and why it is harder to see on mammograms.

Particular features

  • It more often affects several areas of the same breast and the other breast.
  • It is almost always hormone receptor-positive and HER2-negative, with low proliferation.
  • It responds less well to chemotherapy before surgery than carcinoma of no special type.
  • When it spreads, it can go to unusual sites: peritoneum, ovaries, digestive tract and meninges, as well as bone.
  • Relapses can be late.
  • An inherited CDH1 variant is linked to hereditary diffuse gastric cancer and lobular breast cancer.

Diagnosis

Mammography and ultrasound, with MRI to measure the extent accurately before surgery in many cases. The biopsy confirms the type by the absence of E-cadherin on immunohistochemistry.

Treatment

It follows the principles for hormone receptor-positive breast cancer:

  • Breast-conserving surgery if clear margins can be achieved; re-operation for involved margins is more common than in other types.
  • Radiotherapy after breast-conserving surgery.
  • Hormone therapy for 5-10 years, the mainstay of treatment, with CDK4/6 inhibitors for high-risk tumours.
  • Chemotherapy only if the risk is high, guided by genomic tests.
  • In metastatic disease, hormone therapy with CDK4/6 inhibitors and the usual targeted treatments.

Lobular carcinoma in situ

A lesion of the lobules not considered cancer in the strict sense, but a risk marker: women who have it have an increased risk of cancer in both breasts. It is usually managed with surveillance and sometimes preventive drugs. Some variants, such as pleomorphic LCIS, are treated like ductal carcinoma in situ.

Outlook

Stage for stage, the outlook is similar to receptor-positive carcinoma of no special type. The first years usually go well, but the risk of late relapse means hormone therapy and follow-up must continue.
