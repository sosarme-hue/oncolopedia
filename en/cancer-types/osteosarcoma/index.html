Cancer types

Osteosarcoma

The most common bone cancer in teenagers. It appears near the knee or shoulder during the growth spurt; with chemotherapy and surgery about two in three localised cases are cured, far fewer when there are metastases.

At a glance
How common 3-4 cases per million a year; peak at 10-20 years and another over 60
Where End of the femur or tibia by the knee, humerus
Sign Persistent bone pain, often at night, and swelling
Treatment Chemotherapy (MAP) before and after limb-sparing surgery
Survival 65-70% without metastases; 20-30% with metastases

What it is

A tumour of cells that make immature bone (osteoid). It appears in fast-growing areas during adolescence. Risk factors: Li-Fraumeni syndrome, hereditary retinoblastoma, previous radiotherapy, Paget disease of bone in older people.

Symptoms

Pain that does not settle over weeks, worse at night and not explained by an injury; swelling or a mass; sometimes a fracture. Growing pains are in both legs and do not persistently wake the child: one-sided persistent pain should be investigated with an X-ray.

Treatment

  1. Biopsy at the reference centre.
  2. MAP chemotherapy (high-dose methotrexate, doxorubicin and cisplatin) for about 10 weeks.
  3. Surgery with wide margins and reconstruction with an endoprosthesis (some extendable in growing children) or grafts; amputation is now uncommon.
  4. Chemotherapy after surgery.
  5. Lung metastases: removal of all of them whenever possible.
  6. At relapse: ifosfamide, regorafenib, cabozantinib; mifamurtide in some countries.

Afterwards

Rehabilitation and physiotherapy; monitoring of hearing (cisplatin), heart (doxorubicin) and kidneys; support for returning to school and social life.

See also: Sarcomas, Ewing sarcoma, Childhood cancer.
