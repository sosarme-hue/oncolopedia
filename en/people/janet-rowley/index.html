Janet Rowley

The American geneticist who discovered in 1973 that the Philadelphia chromosome arises from an exchange between two chromosomes. She showed that cancer is a genetic disease.

Chromosomes on the kitchen table

Janet Davison Rowley qualified in medicine at 23 in Chicago. For years she worked part-time while raising four children. In the 1970s she applied a new technique, chromosome banding with quinacrine and Giemsa, which allowed each chromosome to be told apart in detail. She used to say she analysed cut-out photographs of chromosomes on her kitchen table.

The Philadelphia chromosome explained

In 1960 Peter Nowell and David Hungerford in Philadelphia had found an abnormally short chromosome 22 in the cells of chronic myeloid leukaemia. In 1973 Rowley showed that the missing piece had not been lost: it had been swapped with a piece of chromosome 9. It was the first translocation described in a human cancer. That same year she described another, between chromosomes 8 and 21, in acute myeloid leukaemia.

From genetics to treatment

Many scientists then doubted that chromosome changes caused cancer. Rowley and others found dozens of specific translocations in leukaemias and lymphomas. Years later it emerged that the 9;22 translocation creates the BCR-ABL fusion gene, and in 2001 imatinib, designed against that protein, turned chronic myeloid leukaemia into a controllable disease.

See also: Chronic myeloid leukaemia, Brian Druker, Theodor Boveri.
