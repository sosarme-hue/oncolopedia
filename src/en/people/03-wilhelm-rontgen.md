## A mysterious light

On 8 November 1895, in his laboratory in Würzburg, Wilhelm Röntgen was experimenting with cathode ray tubes covered in black cardboard. He noticed that a screen coated with barium platinocyanide glowed several metres away, although no visible light left the tube. He had discovered a new kind of radiation that could pass through matter, and he called it **X-rays** because he did not know what it was.

## Anna Bertha's hand

Weeks later he X-rayed his wife Anna Bertha's hand: the bones and her ring could be seen. On seeing it, she reportedly said: "I have seen my death." Röntgen's paper was published on 28 December 1895 and the news went round the world in January 1896. Within months hospitals were using X-rays to find fractures and bullets.

## From image to treatment

It was soon noticed that X-rays burned the skin and made hair fall out. Some doctors thought this ability to damage tissue could be used against tumours. By 1896 skin and breast cancers were being treated with X-rays, and in 1899 the first cure of a skin cancer was described. It was the birth of **radiotherapy**. The first generations of radiologists, unprotected, suffered burns, cancers and amputations.

## A generous scientist

Röntgen refused to patent his discovery so that it would be available to all, and donated his Nobel Prize money to the University of Würzburg. He died of bowel cancer in 1923.
