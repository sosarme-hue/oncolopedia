Cancer types

Medullary thyroid cancer

Cancer of the thyroid C cells, inherited in a quarter of cases through RET gene mutations. Identifying carriers allows the thyroid to be removed before it develops.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common 2-3% of thyroid cancers
Origin C (parafollicular) cells, which make calcitonin
Inheritance 25%: MEN2A, MEN2B (RET mutations)
Markers Calcitonin and CEA
Treatment Total thyroidectomy with nodes; selpercatinib or pralsetinib if advanced

What it is

Unlike papillary cancer, it does not take up iodine, so radioactive iodine does not work. It produces calcitonin and CEA, used for diagnosis and follow-up. It can cause diarrhoea and facial flushing when disease is extensive.

Inherited forms

  • MEN2A: medullary carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma and hyperparathyroidism.
  • MEN2B: very early, aggressive medullary carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma, neuromas on lips and tongue, marfanoid build.
  • Isolated familial medullary carcinoma.

RET testing is recommended for every patient. Carrier relatives have a preventive thyroidectomy at an age that depends on the mutation (in the first year of life for MEN2B).

Treatment

  • Total thyroidectomy with central (and lateral if involved) node dissection. Phaeochromocytoma must be ruled out first.
  • Follow-up with calcitonin and CEA: their doubling time indicates how fast the disease is moving.
  • Advanced and progressive: selpercatinib or pralsetinib (selective RET inhibitors), more effective and better tolerated than vandetanib and cabozantinib (LIBRETTO-531 trial).

Outlook

Ten-year survival is over 95% if confined to the thyroid, and 75% with nodes.

See also: Thyroid cancer, Phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma, Inherited risk and genetic counselling.
