Living with cancer

Emotional support

A cancer diagnosis affects the person and the whole family. Emotional reactions are normal, and asking for help is part of treatment.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

Normal reactions to an abnormal situation

Fear, sadness, anger, disbelief, guilt, anxiety about tests, trouble sleeping. These are expected reactions to a cancer diagnosis and do not mean something is wrong. They tend to come and go: there are better and worse days.

About a third of people with cancer at some point have intense distress, anxiety or depression that needs specific attention. Ask for help if sadness or anxiety stops you functioning, lasts for weeks, you enjoy nothing, or you have thoughts that life is not worth living.

Where to find support

  • Your cancer team: doctor, specialist nurse, and psycho-oncologist if your hospital has one.
  • Your family doctor.
  • Cancer charities' helplines: for example Macmillan Cancer Support in the UK (0808 808 00 00) or the NCI Cancer Information Service in the US (1-800-4-CANCER).
  • Patient organisations and support groups, where you can share with people who have been through the same.
  • If you have thoughts of harming yourself, contact a crisis line (such as Samaritans 116 123 in the UK and Ireland, or 988 in the US) or emergency services.

Psycho-oncology

Psycho-oncology is the specialty that addresses the emotional side of cancer. It helps people cope with diagnosis, decisions, treatment, uncertainty, talking with family, returning to normal life or the end of life. Psychological interventions have been shown to reduce anxiety and depression and improve quality of life.

Talking with family and children

Every family communicates in its own way, but generally it helps to talk honestly, adapting information to each person. Children should be told in simple words what is happening, that it is not their fault, that cancer is not catching and who will look after them. Hiding it usually causes more distress.

Carers matter too

Caring for someone with cancer is exhausting. Carers need rest, information and support, and have a right to ask for it. Accepting help from others is not failing.

About "positive thinking"

Staying in good spirits helps people live better, but there is no evidence that attitude cures cancer or that stress causes it. Nobody should feel guilty for being sad or frightened.
