Cancer types

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma

The second most common skin cancer. It appears on sun-exposed skin, sometimes from actinic keratoses. Surgery cures most localised cases, and cemiplimab is an option for advanced ones.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common About 20% of skin cancers; rising with ageing
Who Older people with fair skin and a lot of sun; transplant recipients (65-250 times higher risk)
Precursors Actinic keratoses, Bowen disease (carcinoma in situ)
Appearance A firm lump or crust growing over weeks, sometimes bleeding or painful
Treatment Surgery; radiotherapy; cemiplimab for advanced tumours

What it is

It arises from keratinocytes, the main cells of the epidermis. Unlike basal cell carcinoma, it can spread to lymph nodes (2-5%), mainly large, deep, lip or ear tumours, or in people with weakened immunity.

Precursor lesions

  • Actinic keratoses: rough scaly patches on the face, bald scalp and backs of hands. Treated with cryotherapy, creams (5-FU, imiquimod, tirbanibulin) or photodynamic therapy.
  • Bowen disease: a red scaly plaque.
  • Actinic cheilitis on the lower lip.

Risk factors

Accumulated sun, age, fair skin, weakened immunity (organ transplants, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia), HPV, chronic scars or burns (Marjolin ulcer), radiotherapy, arsenic and some drugs (voriconazole).

Treatment

  • Surgery with margins or Mohs; cures over 90%.
  • Radiotherapy as an alternative or after surgery for high-risk tumours.
  • Involved nodes: lymphadenectomy and radiotherapy.
  • Advanced or metastatic: cemiplimab (also pembrolizumab or cosibelimab) achieves responses in half of patients, often lasting. In transplant recipients, immunotherapy carries a risk of rejection and must be discussed with the transplant team.
  • Transplant recipients with many tumours: adjusting immunosuppression (sirolimus) and oral acitretin.

See also: Basal cell carcinoma, Melanoma, Mouth cancer.
