## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Frequency** | About 510,000 cases a year worldwide (GLOBOCAN 2022) and about 2,400 in Spain |
| **Types** | Squamous cell carcinoma (smoking, alcohol) and adenocarcinoma (reflux, Barrett's oesophagus, obesity) |
| **Main sign** | Worsening **difficulty swallowing**, first solids then liquids, and weight loss |
| **Diagnosis** | Gastroscopy with biopsy; CT and PET-CT for staging |
| **Treatment** | Chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery; chemoradiotherapy alone for some squamous tumours; immunotherapy in selected cases |
| **Survival** | 13% of men and 16% of women at five years in Spain (net survival, diagnosed 2008-2013, REDECAN registries) |

## What it is

The **oesophagus** is the tube that carries food from the throat to the stomach. Its cancer has **two types** with different causes, location and treatment:

- **Squamous cell carcinoma**: mostly in the upper and middle thirds. Linked to **smoking**, **alcohol** and very hot drinks. It is the most common type worldwide, especially in Asia and East Africa.
- **Adenocarcinoma**: in the lower third, often arising from **Barrett's oesophagus**, a change in the lining caused by chronic **reflux**. Linked to **obesity**, it is the type that has risen most in Europe and North America.

Tumours at the **junction with the stomach** are treated much like [stomach cancer](../stomach-cancer/). To compare both organs, see [Stomach and oesophageal cancer](../stomach-and-oesophageal-cancer/).

## How common it is

It is less common than stomach cancer but among the deadliest, because symptoms usually appear late. It is much more common in men.

## Risk factors

| Factor | Squamous cell | Adenocarcinoma |
|---|---|---|
| **Smoking** | Very important | Yes |
| **Alcohol** | Very important, and multiplies the effect of smoking | Little |
| **Reflux and Barrett's oesophagus** | No | Main cause |
| **Obesity** | No | Yes |
| **Other** | Very hot drinks, achalasia, caustic injury, tylosis (rare) | Male sex, white ethnicity |

## Prevention

- Not smoking and limiting alcohol: the most effective measures against squamous cell carcinoma.
- Keeping a healthy **weight** and treating persistent **reflux**.
- People with **Barrett's oesophagus** have **regular gastroscopies**. If dysplasia appears, it can be treated endoscopically (ablation or resection) before it becomes cancer.

## Symptoms

- **Dysphagia**: difficulty swallowing, with food feeling stuck. First solids, then liquids.
- Unexplained **weight loss**.
- Pain behind the breastbone on swallowing, persistent heartburn.
- **Hoarseness**, coughing when eating or repeated chest infections in advanced disease.

Unexplained difficulty swallowing should be checked soon, even if mild. See [Symptoms not to ignore](../../prevention-and-screening/symptoms-not-to-ignore/).

## Diagnosis

1. **Gastroscopy** with biopsy.
2. Chest and abdominal **CT** and **PET-CT** for staging.
3. **Endoscopic ultrasound** to see depth and lymph nodes.
4. For upper-third tumours, bronchoscopy and examination of the throat.
5. Biomarkers in advanced disease: **PD-L1**, **HER2** (in adenocarcinoma) and microsatellite instability.

## Treatment

**Very early tumours**: endoscopic removal.

**Localised tumours**:
- **Adenocarcinoma**: **FLOT** chemotherapy before and after surgery, preferred after the ESOPEC trial, or chemoradiotherapy (CROSS regimen) followed by surgery.
- **Squamous cell carcinoma**: **chemoradiotherapy** followed by surgery or, in some patients, definitive chemoradiotherapy with close follow-up.
- **Oesophagectomy**, often minimally invasive, in high-volume centres.
- If tumour remains after chemoradiotherapy and surgery, **nivolumab** for a year (CheckMate 577 trial).

**Advanced disease**:
- Platinum and fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy, plus **nivolumab** or **pembrolizumab** depending on PD-L1.
- **Trastuzumab** for HER2-positive adenocarcinomas.
- To help swallowing: a **stent**, radiotherapy or brachytherapy, and nutritional support.

## Outlook

It depends mainly on **stage** at diagnosis. Tumours found very early, often during surveillance of Barrett's oesophagus, do well. Overall, in Spain **13% of men and 16% of women** survive five years (net survival, diagnosed 2008-2013, REDECAN registries). These figures predate current treatments and do not predict an individual case.

## After treatment

After an oesophagectomy people need to **eat small, frequent meals**, slowly and sitting up, avoid lying down after eating and sleep with the head of the bed raised. Reflux, difficulty swallowing from a narrowed join (treated with dilatation) and weight loss are common. A dietitian and a speech and language therapist can help.

## Questions for your team

- Is it squamous cell carcinoma or adenocarcinoma?
- Do you recommend chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy before surgery?
- Could I avoid surgery?
- How many oesophagectomies does the hospital do each year?
- How will I eat during treatment?
