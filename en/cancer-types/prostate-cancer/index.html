Cancer types

Prostate cancer

The most common cancer in men in Europe. Many grow so slowly that they need no immediate treatment; others are aggressive. PSA, MRI and active surveillance help tell them apart.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common 1.5 million cases a year worldwide (the most common in men in Europe)
Who Mostly over 65; more in men of African origin and with family history
Detection Blood PSA and MRI before biopsy
Classification Gleason / ISUP Grade Group (1 to 5), PSA and stage → low, intermediate or high risk
Treatment Active surveillance, prostatectomy, radiotherapy, hormone therapy, new anti-androgens, PSMA-lutetium
Survival Over 90% at five years; almost 100% for localised tumours

What it is

The prostate is a walnut-sized gland below the bladder that produces part of the seminal fluid. 95% of its cancers are acinar adenocarcinomas. Their growth depends on male hormones (androgens), which explains why hormone therapy works.

Many older men have small prostate cancers that will never cause problems: autopsies find them in over a third of men over 70. The big challenge is finding the aggressive ones without overtreating the indolent ones.

Grades

The pathologist uses the Gleason score, now grouped into ISUP Grade Groups:

ISUP group Gleason Behaviour
1 3+3 = 6 Very slow; almost never spreads
2 3+4 = 7 Favourable intermediate
3 4+3 = 7 Unfavourable intermediate
4 8 High risk
5 9-10 Very aggressive

Risk factors

  • Age: rare before 50.
  • Family history: an affected father or brother doubles the risk.
  • African ancestry: more common and more aggressive; in the UK, one in four Black men will get prostate cancer.
  • Inherited mutations: BRCA2 (more aggressive tumours), BRCA1, ATM, HOXB13, Lynch syndrome.
  • Obesity is linked to more aggressive tumours. No diet has been proven to prevent it.

Early detection and PSA

PSA (prostate-specific antigen) is a protein that rises in cancer, but also with benign enlargement or infection.

  • Population PSA screening reduces deaths (European ERSPC trial: 20% fewer deaths at 16 years), but can lead to overdiagnosis.
  • The 2022 EU recommendation proposes organised programmes based on PSA and MRI before biopsy, which reduce unnecessary biopsies and diagnoses of indolent tumours. Pilot programmes run in several countries (PRAISE-U project), and the UK TRANSFORM trial is testing new approaches.
  • Meanwhile, the decision to have a PSA test from 50 (45 with family history, Black ancestry or BRCA2) should be shared and informed with a doctor.

Symptoms

Early tumours cause no symptoms. Urinary problems (weak flow, getting up at night) are usually due to benign enlargement. In advanced stages: blood in urine or semen, bone pain (back, hips), weight loss.

Diagnosis

  1. PSA and digital rectal examination.
  2. Multiparametric MRI (PI-RADS scale) before biopsy.
  3. Targeted biopsy of suspicious areas, preferably transperineal (fewer infections).
  4. Staging in intermediate-high risk: PSMA PET (much more sensitive than CT and bone scan).
  5. Genetic testing in metastatic disease or with family history.

Risk groups and treatment

Low risk (ISUP 1, PSA < 10): - Active surveillance: regular PSA, MRI and repeat biopsies; treatment only if it progresses. It spares most men side effects. The ProtecT trial showed that at 15 years cancer deaths are very low (about 3%) and similar with monitoring, surgery or radiotherapy.

Intermediate and high-risk localised: - Radical prostatectomy (mostly robotic), with or without lymph node removal. - External radiotherapy (including 5-session hypofractionation, PACE-B trial) or brachytherapy, with hormone therapy for 6 months to 2-3 years depending on risk. - In high risk, adding abiraterone to radiotherapy and hormone therapy (STAMPEDE) improves survival.

Biochemical recurrence (PSA rises after treatment): salvage radiotherapy, PSMA PET to locate it, hormone therapy; enzalutamide for high-risk recurrence (EMBARK).

Hormone-sensitive metastatic disease: - Androgen deprivation (LHRH analogues, relugolix) always combined with a new-generation anti-androgen (abiraterone, enzalutamide, apalutamide, darolutamide), sometimes with docetaxel (triplet). - Radiotherapy to the prostate for low-volume metastases.

Castration-resistant disease: - Lutetium-177-PSMA-617 (VISION trial), a radiopharmaceutical that seeks out PSMA-expressing cells. - Olaparib or talazoparib with anti-androgens for BRCA or other repair gene mutations. - Docetaxel, cabazitaxel, radium-223 (bone metastases). - Bone protection with zoledronic acid or denosumab.

Side effects

  • Surgery: incontinence (improves over months in most) and erectile dysfunction.
  • Radiotherapy: urinary and bowel symptoms, gradual erectile dysfunction.
  • Hormone therapy: hot flushes, loss of libido, tiredness, weight gain, loss of muscle and bone, cardiovascular risk. Exercise counters many of them.

Outlook

Over 90% of men are alive at five years. In metastatic disease survival has improved greatly: with today's combined treatments, the median exceeds 5 years for many patients.

Questions for your team

  • What is my risk group?
  • Am I a candidate for active surveillance?
  • How do surgery and radiotherapy compare in my case?
  • Should I have genetic testing? And my relatives?
  • How can I prevent the effects of hormone therapy?
