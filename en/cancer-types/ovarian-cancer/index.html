Cancer types

Ovarian cancer

The deadliest gynaecological cancer, because it is usually found late. Types, symptoms to know, inheritance, surgery, chemotherapy and PARP inhibitors.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common About 325,000 cases a year worldwide
Most common type High-grade serous carcinoma, which almost always starts in the fallopian tube
Symptoms Persistent bloating, feeling full quickly, pelvic pain, needing to pass urine often
Inheritance Up to 20% of high-grade serous carcinomas have variants in BRCA1/2 or other genes
Screening There is no effective screening for the general population
Survival About 45% at five years; over 90% if found at stage I

What it is

"Ovarian cancer" covers very different tumours. The great majority are epithelial carcinomas, which also include cancers of the fallopian tube and primary peritoneal cancer, because they behave and are treated in the same way. We now know that the most common type, high-grade serous, usually begins at the end of the tube, as a precursor lesion called STIC, and then spreads to the ovary and peritoneum.

Germ cell and sex cord-stromal tumours are less common and have their own page.

Types of ovarian carcinoma

Type Share Features
High-grade serous 70% Aggressive, usually diagnosed late, very platinum-sensitive; TP53 almost always mutated; linked to BRCA
Endometrioid 10% Often linked to endometriosis; usually found early
Clear cell 5-10% Linked to endometriosis; less sensitive to chemotherapy
Mucinous 3% Can be very large; a digestive origin must be ruled out
Low-grade serous 5% Slow-growing, in younger women; less sensitive to chemotherapy

Borderline tumours (low malignant potential) are another category, with an excellent outlook and more conservative surgery.

Risk and protective factors

  • Age: most cases after menopause.
  • Inherited variants: BRCA1 (lifetime risk 40-45%), BRCA2 (15-20%), Lynch syndrome genes, RAD51C, RAD51D and BRIP1.
  • Endometriosis (for endometrioid and clear cell types).
  • Never having been pregnant.
  • Protective: the contraceptive pill (reduces risk by up to 50% after several years of use), pregnancies, breastfeeding, tubal ligation and salpingectomy (removing the tubes).

Because of the tubal origin theory, many guidelines recommend offering opportunistic salpingectomy when a woman has gynaecological surgery for another reason and does not want more children.

Symptoms

Ovarian cancer is not silent, but its symptoms are non-specific. See a doctor if they occur almost daily for more than two or three weeks:

  • Bloating or an increase in waist size.
  • Feeling full quickly or loss of appetite.
  • Pain in the pelvis or abdomen.
  • Needing to pass urine more often or urgently.
  • Also tiredness, changes in bowel habit or back pain.

Why there is no screening

Large trials using transvaginal ultrasound and the CA-125 marker, such as the UK's UKCTOCS with over 200,000 women, did not reduce deaths. So screening is not recommended for women without raised risk. BRCA carriers should consider risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy, the most effective measure, usually between 35 and 45 depending on the gene.

Diagnosis

  1. Pelvic examination and transvaginal ultrasound, assessed with systems such as O-RADS or IOTA.
  2. Blood CA-125, sometimes with HE4 (ROMA index). They do not diagnose on their own.
  3. CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis to assess spread.
  4. Histological confirmation: by guided biopsy or at surgery. Sometimes a laparoscopy is done to judge whether the tumour can be removed.
  5. Genetic testing for all patients with non-mucinous epithelial carcinoma, and tumour testing for homologous recombination deficiency (HRD).

Stages (FIGO)

  • I: confined to the ovaries or tubes.
  • II: spread to other pelvic organs.
  • III: spread to the abdominal peritoneum or retroperitoneal nodes (the most common at diagnosis).
  • IV: distant spread, such as malignant pleural effusion, liver or spleen.

Treatment

Surgery: crucial, and should be done by a gynaecological oncologist in an experienced centre. The aim is complete cytoreduction: leaving no visible tumour. In early stages it includes full staging. If surgery is not possible at first, three cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy are given before an operation (interval surgery).

Chemotherapy: carboplatin and paclitaxel, usually six cycles. In some stage III cases with interval surgery, hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) has shown benefit.

Maintenance: after response to chemotherapy, this changes the outlook:

  • PARP inhibitors (olaparib, niraparib), especially in BRCA-mutated or HRD-positive tumours. In the SOLO-1 trial of olaparib in BRCA carriers, almost half had still not needed further treatment at seven years, compared with one in five on placebo.
  • Bevacizumab, alone or combined with olaparib.

Relapse: classified by whether the disease is platinum-sensitive. Options include platinum-based regimens again, other chemotherapy, bevacizumab, surgery in selected cases and new drugs such as mirvetuximab soravtansine, an antibody-drug conjugate for tumours with high folate receptor alpha.

Outlook

Overall five-year survival is about 45%, because three in four cases are diagnosed at stage III-IV. At stage I it exceeds 90%. PARP inhibitors mean that some women with advanced disease and BRCA mutations are alive and disease-free many years later.

Questions for your team

  • Will I be operated on by an experienced gynaecological oncologist?
  • Can all the tumour be removed at the start?
  • What are my BRCA and HRD results?
  • Is maintenance treatment right for me?
  • Should my relatives be tested?
