Cancer types

Ovarian cancer

A cancer with vague symptoms that is often diagnosed late. Variants in BRCA1 and BRCA2 explain some cases and have opened new treatment options.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common About 320,000 cases worldwide in 2022
Most common type High-grade serous carcinoma, which often starts in the fallopian tube
Signs Persistent bloating, feeling full quickly, pelvic pain, needing to pass urine often
Inheritance About 15-20% of high-grade serous carcinomas are linked to inherited variants, mainly in BRCA1 and BRCA2
Screening No effective screening for the general population

What it is

"Ovarian cancer" covers a group of tumours. The most common and aggressive is high-grade serous carcinoma, which is now known often to start in the fallopian tube and spread early across the peritoneum. Other types (endometrioid, clear cell, mucinous, low-grade) and the germ cell tumours of young women behave differently.

Symptoms

They are non-specific, which is why it has been called "the cancer that whispers": bloating or abdominal swelling lasting weeks, feeling full quickly, pelvic or abdominal pain and urinary or bowel changes. If these symptoms are new and occur almost daily for more than a few weeks, see a doctor.

Risk factors

Age, family history of ovarian or breast cancer, variants in BRCA1, BRCA2 and other genes, and Lynch syndrome. The contraceptive pill, pregnancy and breastfeeding lower the risk. Women with high-risk variants are offered removal of the tubes and ovaries once they have completed their families, because there is no reliable screening.

Diagnosis

Examination, transvaginal ultrasound, the CA-125 marker and CT. Diagnosis is confirmed by surgery or biopsy. All women with epithelial ovarian cancer should be offered genetic testing, because of the implications for their treatment and their relatives.

Treatment

  • Cytoreductive surgery, aiming to leave no visible disease. It should be done in experienced centres.
  • Chemotherapy with carboplatin and paclitaxel, before or after surgery.
  • Maintenance with PARP inhibitors (olaparib, niraparib) in tumours with BRCA or homologous recombination changes, sometimes with bevacizumab. They have greatly reduced the risk of relapse in these patients.

Outlook

It depends on stage and how complete the surgery is. Most cases are diagnosed once the disease has spread within the abdomen, but many women achieve long periods without disease.

See also: Inherited risk and genetic counselling, Targeted therapy.
