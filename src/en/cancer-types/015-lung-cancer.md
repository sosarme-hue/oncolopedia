## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | 2.5 million cases a year worldwide (the most common) |
| **Deaths** | 1.8 million a year: almost one in five cancer deaths |
| **Main cause** | **Tobacco** (80-85%); also radon, asbestos, air pollution |
| **Types** | Non-small cell (85%): adenocarcinoma, squamous; small cell (15%) |
| **Detection** | Yearly low-dose CT screening in high-risk people |
| **Treatment** | Surgery, stereotactic radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy |
| **Survival** | Over 60-80% at stage I; about 20-25% at five years overall |

## What it is

Lung cancer starts in the cells lining the **bronchi** and **alveoli**. It grows without symptoms for a long time, so more than half of cases are diagnosed with metastases. It is divided into two main groups with different treatments:

- **Non-small cell lung cancer** (NSCLC), 85%.
- **Small cell lung cancer** (SCLC), 15%, very aggressive and almost exclusive to smokers. It has [its own sheet](../small-cell-lung-cancer/).

## Types of non-small cell cancer

| Type | Share | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Adenocarcinoma** | 50% | The most common, also in never-smokers and women; in the outer lung; often with treatable mutations |
| **Squamous cell carcinoma** | 25% | Tobacco-linked; in central airways; may cause coughing up blood |
| **Large cell carcinoma** | 5-10% | Undifferentiated, fast-growing |

Other chest tumours are [carcinoids](../lung-carcinoid-tumours/), [mesothelioma](../mesothelioma/) and metastases from other cancers, which are more common than primary lung tumours.

## How common it is

It is the most diagnosed cancer in the world and the leading cause of cancer death. It is rising in women in many countries, who took up smoking in large numbers later than men. Between 10 and 25% of cases occur in people who have **never smoked**, especially women and people of East Asian origin.

## Risk factors

- **Tobacco**: multiplies risk 15-30 times. Risk depends on pack-years and falls steadily after quitting, at any age. "Light" cigarettes, cigars and pipes also raise it.
- **Second-hand smoke**: raises risk by 20-30%.
- **Radon**: a natural radioactive gas that builds up in basements and ground floors in granite areas. It is the **leading cause in never-smokers**.
- **Asbestos**, silica, diesel exhaust, arsenic, chromium and other workplace carcinogens.
- **Air pollution** and household smoke from solid fuels.
- Previous chest radiotherapy, lung fibrosis, COPD, family history.

## Prevention and screening

- **Not smoking and quitting** is by far the most effective measure. Quitting before 40 removes almost all the excess risk.
- Testing homes for radon in risk areas and ventilating.
- **Low-dose CT screening**: the NLST (United States) and NELSON (Netherlands and Belgium) trials showed that yearly CT in people aged 50-80 with heavy smoking histories reduces lung cancer deaths by 20-25%. The USA has offered it since 2013, the UK rolled out its national Lung Cancer Screening Programme from 2023, and the EU recommended it in 2022. It must be done in organised programmes that include help to stop smoking.

## Symptoms

They tend to appear late:

- A persistent or changing **cough** in a smoker.
- Coughing up blood (haemoptysis).
- Breathlessness, chest pain, hoarseness.
- Chest infections that keep coming back or do not clear.
- Tiredness, weight and appetite loss.
- Symptoms of spread: bone pain, headache, seizures.
- Special syndromes: swelling of the face and neck (superior vena cava syndrome), drooping eyelid with a small pupil (Horner syndrome, in Pancoast tumour), finger clubbing.

## Diagnosis

1. **Chest X-ray** and, above all, contrast **CT of the chest**.
2. **PET-CT** to assess spread and lymph nodes.
3. **Biopsy** by bronchoscopy, **EBUS** (endobronchial ultrasound, which allows sampling of mediastinal nodes) or CT-guided needle.
4. **Brain MRI** for advanced tumours or candidates for curative treatment.
5. **Molecular profiling**: essential for all advanced adenocarcinomas and many early ones. Tests include *EGFR*, *ALK*, *ROS1*, *KRAS* G12C, *BRAF*, *MET*, *RET*, *NTRK*, *HER2* and the **PD-L1** protein, using next-generation sequencing (NGS) on tissue or **liquid biopsy**.
6. Lung function tests before surgery.

## Stages

The **TNM** system is used (8th edition, with the 9th being introduced from 2025):

| Stage | Simplified description |
|---|---|
| **I** | Small tumour without nodes |
| **II** | Larger tumour or nodes near the lung |
| **III** | Mediastinal nodes or tumour invading neighbouring structures |
| **IV** | Spread to the other lung, the pleura or other organs |

## Treatment

**Early stages (I-II)**:
- **Surgery**: lobectomy with lymph node removal, increasingly by **keyhole (VATS)** or robotic surgery. For small outer tumours, **segmentectomy** is equally effective (JCOG0802 trial) and saves more lung.
- **Stereotactic radiotherapy (SABR)** in a few sessions when surgery is not possible, with 90% local control.
- Additional treatment: **osimertinib** after surgery for *EGFR* mutations (ADAURA trial, with improved overall survival), **alectinib** for *ALK*, and chemotherapy with or without immunotherapy.

**Stage III (locally advanced)**:
- **Chemo-immunotherapy before surgery** (neoadjuvant, with nivolumab, pembrolizumab or durvalumab) for operable tumours: in a quarter, no tumour remains in the specimen.
- If inoperable, **chemoradiotherapy** followed by a year of **durvalumab** (PACIFIC trial), or osimertinib for *EGFR* (LAURA trial).

**Stage IV**:
- With a **treatable molecular alteration**: specific targeted tablets, such as osimertinib (with or without chemotherapy) or amivantamab with lazertinib for *EGFR*; alectinib or **lorlatinib** for *ALK*; repotrectinib or crizotinib for *ROS1*; sotorasib or adagrasib for *KRAS* G12C; dabrafenib with trametinib for *BRAF*; selpercatinib for *RET*; capmatinib or tepotinib for *MET*; trastuzumab deruxtecan for *HER2*. Some people live many years on these treatments.
- Without a treatable alteration: **immunotherapy** alone if PD-L1 is high (≥50%) or **chemo-immunotherapy**. Some patients treated with pembrolizumab are alive at five years, unthinkable fifteen years ago.
- Palliative **radiotherapy**, local treatment of single metastases (oligometastases) and **early palliative care**, which improves quality of life and may prolong it.

## Outlook

Survival has improved more in the last ten years than in the previous forty. At five years: 60-90% at stage I depending on size, 35-50% at stage II, 15-35% at stage III and about 5-10% at stage IV, although much higher for *ALK* or *EGFR* tumours or those responding to immunotherapy.

## After treatment

- CT check-ups every 6 months in the first years.
- **Stopping smoking** improves survival even after diagnosis.
- Pulmonary rehabilitation after surgery.
- Watch for immunotherapy side effects (thyroid, lung, bowel, skin), which can appear months later.

## What's new

- **Antibody-drug conjugates** (datopotamab deruxtecan, sacituzumab) and **bispecific antibodies** (amivantamab, ivonescimab).
- Next-generation and "pan-mutant" *KRAS* inhibitors.
- CT screening assisted by **artificial intelligence**.
- Liquid biopsy to detect residual disease after surgery.

## Questions for your team

- What type of cancer do I have and what stage is it?
- Has full molecular profiling and PD-L1 testing been done?
- Is surgery or curative radiotherapy possible?
- Is there a clinical trial for me?
- What help is there to stop smoking?
