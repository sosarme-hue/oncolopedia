## Why fertility is affected

Chemotherapy, radiotherapy to the pelvis or brain, hormone therapy and some surgery can reduce or remove the ability to have children, temporarily or permanently. The risk depends on the treatment, dose and age. That is why guidelines recommend **discussing fertility before treatment starts** with everyone of reproductive age, and with the parents of children and teenagers.

## Preservation options

**For men**

- **Sperm banking**, simple and effective, before treatment begins.
- For boys who do not yet produce sperm, freezing testicular tissue is experimental.

**For women**

- **Egg freezing**: needs about two weeks of ovarian stimulation, which can often fit around the start of treatment. Adapted protocols are used for hormone-sensitive tumours.
- **Embryo freezing**, with a partner.
- **Ovarian tissue freezing**, which needs no stimulation and can be done in girls; it is reimplanted later.
- Drugs that **protect the ovaries** during chemotherapy (GnRH analogues), as an addition.
- Surgery that **preserves the womb or ovaries** in some early gynaecological cancers.

Access and funding vary between countries and health systems. Ask your oncologist about a referral to a fertility unit as early as possible.

## Pregnancy after cancer

Many people have children after cancer. The right timing depends on the tumour and treatment. In hormone-sensitive breast cancer, an international study showed that temporarily pausing hormone therapy to try for a pregnancy did not increase short-term relapses.

## Cancer during pregnancy

It is uncommon. Many cancers can be treated during pregnancy, with surgery and some chemotherapy from the second trimester, by experienced teams.
