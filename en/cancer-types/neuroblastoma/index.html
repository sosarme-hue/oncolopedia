Cancer types

Neuroblastoma

The most common solid tumour outside the brain in young children. Some disappear on their own and others are very aggressive; anti-GD2 immunotherapy has improved the outlook for high-risk disease.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common 7-8% of childhood cancers; half before age 2
Origin Neural crest cells of the sympathetic nervous system
Where Adrenal gland and abdomen, chest, neck, pelvis
Markers Urinary catecholamines, MYCN amplification, ALK mutations
Treatment From observation to intensive chemotherapy, transplant, radiotherapy, anti-GD2 and isotretinoin
Survival > 90% for low risk; 50-60% for high risk

A tumour of extremes

  • In infants, some tumours (stage MS) regress on their own without treatment, even with liver and skin lesions.
  • High-risk tumours (MYCN amplification, metastases over 18 months of age) are among the hardest childhood cancers.

Symptoms

Abdominal mass, bone pain, limping, purple bruising around the eyes ("raccoon eyes") from orbital metastases, fever, anaemia; sometimes opsoclonus-myoclonus syndrome (rapid eye movements and jerks), diarrhoea or high blood pressure.

High-risk treatment

Induction chemotherapy, surgery, high-dose chemotherapy with autologous transplant, radiotherapy, and anti-GD2 antibody immunotherapy (dinutuximab beta) with isotretinoin. Also therapeutic MIBG, ALK inhibitors (lorlatinib) for mutated tumours, and eflornithine (DFMO) maintenance, approved in the USA in 2023.

See also: Childhood cancer, Wilms tumour, Phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma.
