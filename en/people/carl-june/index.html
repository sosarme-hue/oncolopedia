Carl June

The American immunologist who brought CAR-T cells, a patient's lymphocytes modified to attack their cancer, to the clinic. His first child patient, Emily Whitehead, remains in remission.

Reprogrammed lymphocytes

Carl June, a military doctor and researcher at the University of Pennsylvania, worked on how to grow T lymphocytes in the laboratory to treat HIV. After his wife died of ovarian cancer in 2001, he turned his work to cancer. His team genetically modified patients' T cells to carry a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) that recognises the protein CD19, found on the surface of B cells in leukaemias and lymphomas.

The first patients

In 2011 they reported results in three patients with resistant chronic lymphocytic leukaemia: two had complete remissions. In 2012 they treated Emily Whitehead, a 6-year-old with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia that had relapsed twice. She had a severe reaction, cytokine release syndrome, which was controlled with tocilizumab. She went into remission and more than a decade later remains well.

A living drug

In 2017 the FDA approved tisagenlecleucel, the first CAR-T therapy, for childhood acute lymphoblastic leukaemia. There are now several CAR-T therapies for lymphomas, leukaemias and myeloma, and they are being studied in solid tumours and autoimmune diseases.

See also: Stem cell transplant and CAR-T, Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, Steven Rosenberg.
