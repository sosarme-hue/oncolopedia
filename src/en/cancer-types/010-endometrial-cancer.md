## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | About 420,000 cases a year worldwide |
| **Who** | Mostly women over 55, after menopause |
| **Typical sign** | **Any vaginal bleeding after menopause** |
| **Risk factors** | Obesity, excess oestrogen, diabetes, Lynch syndrome, tamoxifen |
| **Treatment** | Hysterectomy with tubes and ovaries; radiotherapy, chemotherapy and immunotherapy depending on risk |
| **Survival** | About 75-80% at five years; over 90% at stage I |

## What it is

The **endometrium** is the lining of the womb that renews with each period. Endometrial cancer starts in its glands. It is different from cervical cancer and from **uterine sarcomas**, which start in the muscle and are much rarer.

Its incidence is **rising** in many countries because of ageing and obesity, and it is one of the few cancers whose death rate is not falling.

## Types

Classically two groups were distinguished:

- **Endometrioid** (80%): oestrogen-related, usually low grade and found early.
- **Non-endometrioid**: serous, clear cell, carcinosarcoma and undifferentiated. More aggressive and more common in older women.

Since 2020-2023, the WHO and FIGO include the **molecular classification**, which predicts the outlook better:

| Molecular group | Share | Outlook |
|---|---|---|
| **POLE ultramutated** | 5-10% | Excellent; often needs no further treatment after surgery |
| **Mismatch repair deficient (dMMR / MSI)** | 25-30% | Intermediate; responds very well to immunotherapy; Lynch syndrome must be ruled out |
| **p53 abnormal** | 10-15% | Unfavourable; benefits from chemotherapy |
| **No specific molecular profile (NSMP)** | 40-50% | Intermediate; hormone receptors matter |

## Risk factors

Most relate to exposure of the endometrium to **oestrogen without enough progesterone**:

- **Obesity**: fat tissue makes oestrogen. It is the most important factor; risk multiplies with body mass index.
- Diabetes, polycystic ovary syndrome, late menopause, never having children.
- Oestrogen-only hormone therapy without progesterone, and **tamoxifen**.
- **Lynch syndrome**: 30-60% lifetime risk depending on the gene.
- Protective: the contraceptive pill, the levonorgestrel IUD, physical activity and pregnancies.

## Symptoms

**Any bleeding after menopause** should be checked, however slight or even once: about 5-10% is due to cancer. In younger women, very heavy periods, bleeding between periods or persistent bleeding. Also abnormal discharge and, in advanced stages, pelvic pain.

## Diagnosis

1. **Transvaginal ultrasound**: a thin endometrium (under 3-4 mm) after menopause makes cancer very unlikely.
2. **Endometrial biopsy**: with a thin tube in clinic or by **hysteroscopy**.
3. **Molecular classification** and receptor testing on the biopsy.
4. Pelvic **MRI** to see how deep it grows into the muscle, and **CT** of chest and abdomen.
5. Testing of mismatch repair proteins and, if missing, **genetic counselling** for Lynch syndrome.

## Treatment

**Surgery**: **total hysterectomy with removal of tubes and ovaries**, usually by **keyhole** or robotic surgery, with lymph node assessment using the **sentinel node**. In young women with very early tumours, keeping the ovaries may be considered.

**Treatment after surgery**, according to risk group, which combines stage, type and molecular profile:

- **Low risk**: follow-up only.
- **Intermediate**: **vaginal brachytherapy**, which reduces relapses at the top of the vagina with few side effects.
- **High risk**: pelvic radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy (carboplatin and paclitaxel).
- **Advanced or recurrent**: chemotherapy with **immunotherapy** (dostarlimab, pembrolizumab or durvalumab), which in dMMR tumours very markedly reduces the risk of progression (RUBY and NRG-GY018 trials). At relapse, pembrolizumab with lenvatinib, hormone therapy (progestogens) or trastuzumab for HER2-positive serous tumours.

**Preserving fertility**: in young women with low-grade endometrioid tumours confined to the endometrium, **progestogens** (oral or IUD) can be used with regular biopsies, completing surgery after pregnancies.

## Outlook

Because most are diagnosed at stage I, overall five-year survival is about 75-80%, and over 90% for low-grade stage I. Serous and p53-abnormal types have a worse outlook.

## After treatment

Check-ups every 3-6 months in the first years, with vaginal examination. Losing weight and exercising improves overall health, as many of these women are at higher risk from cardiovascular disease than from relapse. Brachytherapy and radiotherapy can cause vaginal dryness and narrowing, prevented with dilators.
