## A patient at Johns Hopkins

Henrietta Lacks was a 30-year-old African American woman, a mother of five, who had worked in tobacco farming in Virginia and lived in Baltimore. In 1951 she went to Johns Hopkins Hospital, one of the few that treated Black patients, with vaginal bleeding. She was diagnosed with a very aggressive **cervical cancer**. She died eight months later.

## Immortal cells

During her treatment, a sample of the tumour was taken **without telling her or asking her permission**, as was common then. The researcher George Gey had spent years trying to keep human cells alive in the laboratory. Henrietta's not only survived: they multiplied without stopping. He called them **HeLa**, after the first letters of her name.

## An involuntary gift to science

HeLa cells were shared around the world. They were used to develop the **polio vaccine**, to study cancer, viruses, radiation and the effects of thousands of drugs, and to discover how **HPV** causes cervical cancer: HeLa cells contain HPV 18. Tens of tonnes are estimated to have been grown and they appear in more than 100,000 publications.

## An ethical debt

Her family knew nothing for more than 20 years and received no benefit. Her story, told in Rebecca Skloot's book *The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks* (2010), changed the debate on **informed consent** and genetic privacy. In 2013 the US National Institutes of Health reached an agreement with the family on access to her genome, and in 2021 the WHO honoured her.
