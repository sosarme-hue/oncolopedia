Precision medicine

Choosing treatment according to the molecular features of each tumour. What already works, its limits and why it does not mean tailor-made treatment for everyone.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

The idea

For decades cancer was treated according to the organ where it started. Precision medicine adds another question: which molecular changes drive this particular tumour? If one of them is a treatable target, a drug aimed at it can be chosen.

What already works

  • In advanced non-small cell lung cancer, patients with changes in EGFR, ALK, ROS1 and other genes take targeted tablets that have multiplied their survival.
  • In breast cancer, HER2 and hormone receptors have guided treatment for decades.
  • In chronic myeloid leukaemia and GIST, imatinib.
  • In melanoma, BRAF and MEK inhibitors.
  • Tumour-agnostic treatments, approved for a change whatever the organ: NTRK inhibitors, or immunotherapy for tumours with microsatellite instability.

Its limits

  • Only some patients have a change with an effective treatment available. Large studies estimate that sequencing changes treatment in a minority of cases, though the share is growing.
  • Tumours are heterogeneous and evolve: resistance appears.
  • The same change can respond differently depending on the organ (for example BRAF in melanoma and in colon cancer).
  • To rank the evidence, ESMO created the ESCAT scale, which grades each change by what has been proven.

Beyond DNA

Precision includes other data: protein expression, the tumour's immune environment, liquid biopsy, pharmacogenetics (how each person processes drugs, such as testing DPYD before giving fluoropyrimidines) and, increasingly, artificial intelligence applied to images and data.
