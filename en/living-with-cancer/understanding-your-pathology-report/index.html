Understanding your pathology report

The pathology report confirms the diagnosis and guides treatment. What its sections are, what the most common terms mean and what to ask.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

What it is and who writes it

When a sample is taken (a biopsy or tissue removed at surgery), it is examined by a pathologist. Their report confirms whether there is cancer, what type it is, how aggressive it looks and what features it has. Almost every treatment decision rests on it.

It is written for other doctors, so it is normal not to understand it. You can ask for a copy: it is part of your medical record. Read it with your team, not alone with a search engine.

The sections of the report

Section What it tells you
Specimen details Where the sample came from, how it was taken (needle biopsy, endoscopy, surgery) and its size
Gross (macroscopic) description What can be seen by eye: tumour size, appearance, distance to the edges
Microscopic description What the cells look like under the microscope
Additional tests Immunohistochemistry, biomarkers and molecular studies
Diagnosis The conclusion, usually at the end in a few lines. The most important part
Comments Nuances, uncertainties or pending tests

Common terms

  • Benign / malignant: a benign tumour does not invade or spread; a malignant one is a cancer.
  • Carcinoma, sarcoma, lymphoma, melanoma: the tissue the cancer comes from (linings, supporting tissues, lymph nodes, pigment cells).
  • In situ: cancer cells have not broken through the layer where they started. Invasive or infiltrating: they have.
  • Grade (1, 2, 3 or low/high): how different the cells look from normal and how fast they grow. A high grade is usually more aggressive. It is not the same as stage, which measures spread.
  • Margins: whether the edge of the removed tissue is free of tumour (negative or clear) or not (positive). A positive margin may need more surgery or radiotherapy.
  • Lymphovascular or perineural invasion: tumour cells inside vessels or nerves. It raises the risk of relapse.
  • Lymph nodes: how many were examined and how many contain cancer (for example, "2/15").
  • pTNM: the stage based on the surgical specimen; the "p" means pathological. See Stage and grade.
  • Ki-67: the percentage of cells that are dividing.
  • Receptors, HER2, PD-L1, MSI, mutations: biomarkers showing whether a specific treatment may work. See Biomarkers and molecular profiling.

Worth knowing

  • Sometimes a preliminary report comes first and another follows days later with molecular results. Your team may wait for everything before deciding.
  • A small biopsy may not represent the whole tumour; the surgical report can change the grade or stage.
  • Difficult cases are often reviewed by another pathologist. You can also ask for a second opinion: see Shared decisions and second opinions.

Questions for your team

  • Exactly what type of cancer is it and what grade?
  • Was all of it removed? What are the margins?
  • Are any lymph nodes affected?
  • Are any results still pending?
  • Which biomarkers change my treatment?
