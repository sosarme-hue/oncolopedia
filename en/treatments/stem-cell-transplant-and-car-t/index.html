Stem cell transplant and CAR-T

Blood stem cell transplants and CAR-T therapies are intensive treatments for some leukaemias, lymphomas and myelomas. What they are and how they work.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

Blood stem cell transplant

Often called a bone marrow transplant. It means giving blood stem cells (haematopoietic stem cells) to a person after intensive chemotherapy, sometimes with radiotherapy, that destroys their marrow. The new cells travel to the bones and start making blood again.

There are two types:

  • Autologous: the patient's own cells, collected before treatment. This allows very high doses of chemotherapy. Used mainly in myeloma and some lymphomas.
  • Allogeneic: cells from a donor, related or unrelated. As well as restoring the marrow, the new immune system attacks any remaining malignant cells (the graft-versus-leukaemia effect). Used in high-risk acute leukaemias and other diseases. Its main complication is graft-versus-host disease, in which donor cells attack the patient's tissues.

Cells are now usually collected from peripheral blood, after stimulating the marrow with a drug; sometimes from bone marrow or umbilical cord blood.

Becoming a donor

National registries, linked through the World Marrow Donor Association, search for unrelated donors worldwide. Examples are Anthony Nolan and DKMS in the UK and NMDP (Be The Match) in the US. Healthy young adults can join; age limits vary by registry. Donating almost never requires an operation.

CAR-T therapies

In CAR-T, a patient's T lymphocytes are collected and, in a laboratory, given a gene that makes a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR): an "antenna" that recognises a protein on malignant cells, such as CD19 or BCMA. The cells are multiplied and infused back, acting as a living drug.

They are approved for some acute lymphoblastic leukaemias, B-cell lymphomas and myelomas that have relapsed or not responded to other treatments, and can achieve lasting remissions in patients with no other options. They are given in accredited hospitals.

Their most important effects are cytokine release syndrome (fever, low blood pressure) and neurological toxicity (confusion, speech problems), which are monitored and treated in hospital.

CAR-T is being studied for solid tumours, autoimmune diseases and "off-the-shelf" versions from donors.
