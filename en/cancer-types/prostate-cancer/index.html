Cancer types

Prostate cancer

The most common cancer in men. Many grow so slowly they would never cause harm, while others need treatment. That is why diagnosis and PSA testing call for an informed conversation.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common About 1.5 million cases worldwide in 2022
Who Men, mostly over 60
Signs Often none. Urinary symptoms are usually due to benign enlargement
PSA A useful but imperfect blood test: it can rise without cancer and miss some cancers
Survival Around 90% at five years in Europe

What it is

This is cancer of the prostate, the male gland below the bladder that produces part of the seminal fluid. Almost all are adenocarcinomas. Their behaviour varies widely: many are low risk and grow so slowly that they do not shorten life, while others are aggressive.

Risk factors

Age, family history (father or brothers), Black African ancestry and inherited variants in genes such as BRCA2. There are no proven prevention measures beyond a healthy lifestyle.

Symptoms

Early on it almost never causes symptoms. Difficulty or frequency passing urine is very common after 50 and is usually due to benign prostatic hyperplasia. Advanced disease can cause bone pain, blood in the urine or semen and tiredness.

PSA and screening

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is measured in the blood. It can be raised by enlargement, infection, inflammation or even cycling, and it can be normal when cancer is present. Testing every man reduces deaths somewhat, but leads to diagnosing and treating many tumours that would never have caused problems (overdiagnosis), with side effects such as incontinence or erectile dysfunction.

That is why most countries, including Spain and the UK, do not have population screening. The European Union recommends exploring organised programmes that combine PSA with MRI to reduce unnecessary biopsies. If you are thinking about a PSA test, discuss the pros and cons with your doctor.

Diagnosis

A raised PSA is usually followed by multiparametric MRI and, if there is a suspicious area, a biopsy. The pathologist assigns a Gleason score (grouped into ISUP grades 1 to 5) that reflects aggressiveness. PSA and extent are used to classify risk.

Treatment

  • Low risk: active surveillance, with regular PSA, MRI and biopsies. Treatment starts only if the tumour changes. It avoids side effects without worsening survival.
  • Intermediate or high risk: radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy, often with hormone therapy (androgen deprivation).
  • Advanced disease: hormone therapy combined with newer anti-androgens, chemotherapy, radiopharmaceuticals or PARP inhibitors in tumours with BRCA changes.

Outlook

Most men diagnosed will not die from this cancer. Even with metastases, many live for years with good quality of life.
