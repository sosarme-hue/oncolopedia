**1 December** is World AIDS Day. It is a good moment to recall a story that ties HIV and oncology closely together: the epidemic was discovered, in large part, through a cancer.

## A rare cancer that raised the alarm

In 1872, the Hungarian dermatologist **Moritz Kaposi** described, in Vienna, several cases of a skin tumour with violet-coloured patches in older men. For a century, [Kaposi sarcoma](../../cancer-types/kaposi-sarcoma/) was a rarity seen mainly in older men of Mediterranean or Eastern European origin, in some parts of Africa and in transplant recipients taking immunosuppressive drugs.

In July 1981, the bulletin of the US Centers for Disease Control (CDC) reported something puzzling: within a few months, **26 cases of Kaposi sarcoma** had been diagnosed in young men in New York and California, some of them also with a very rare pneumonia. It was one of the first signs that something was destroying these people's immune systems. Two years later the responsible virus, HIV, was identified.

## A virus behind the tumour

One piece was missing. Why did Kaposi sarcoma appear in people with weakened immunity? In 1994, **Yuan Chang and Patrick Moore** found in these tumours the DNA of a new virus from the herpes family, **human herpesvirus 8** (HHV-8 or KSHV). We now know this virus is needed for the tumour to develop, and that a healthy immune system usually keeps it in check. It is one of the clearest examples of [cancer caused by an infection](../../people/harald-zur-hausen/), alongside human papillomavirus and the hepatitis viruses.

## What treatment changed

In the mid-1990s **combination antiretroviral therapy** arrived. By controlling HIV and restoring immunity, it led to a dramatic fall in Kaposi sarcoma and in the aggressive **lymphomas** linked to AIDS in countries with access to treatment. Kaposi sarcoma often improves with HIV treatment alone, although chemotherapy is sometimes added.

The **START** trial, published in 2015, also showed that starting antiretroviral therapy **as soon as the infection is diagnosed**, without waiting for immunity to fall, reduces serious complications, including cancers. That is why everyone with HIV is now advised to start treatment at diagnosis.

## Today's map: less AIDS, other cancers

People with well-treated HIV now have a life expectancy close to that of the general population. That means they grow older, and with age come the common cancers. They also remain at higher risk of some tumours:

- **Cancers linked to human papillomavirus:** [cervical cancer](../../cancer-types/cervical-cancer/) and, above all, [anal cancer](../../cancer-types/anal-cancer/), which is much more common than in the general population.
- **Lymphomas**, both [non-Hodgkin lymphomas](../../cancer-types/lymphomas/) and [Hodgkin lymphoma](../../cancer-types/hodgkin-lymphoma/).
- **Liver cancer**, when there is also hepatitis B or C infection.
- **Lung cancer**, largely because smoking is more common.

## Prevention is possible

Much of this risk can be reduced:

- **Diagnose HIV early and treat it** from the start.
- **Get vaccinated against human papillomavirus** and hepatitis B, following the recommendations for people with HIV.
- **Cervical screening**, which is done more often in women with HIV. See [Prevention and screening](../../prevention-and-screening/).
- **Checks for anal lesions.** The **ANCHOR** trial, published in 2022, showed that finding and treating precancerous anal lesions in people with HIV more than halves the risk of anal cancer.
- **Stopping smoking**, which brings a particularly large benefit for people with HIV.

Kaposi sarcoma went from being the warning sign of an epidemic to a much rarer disease, thanks to research and treatment. But in countries where access to treatment is still limited, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, it remains one of the most common cancers. If you have HIV, your HIV clinic will tell you which check-ups apply to you.
