## The initial blow

Hearing the word "cancer" is one of the hardest moments a person can go through. In the first days it is common to feel:

- **Shock** or unreality: "this can't be happening to me".
- **Fear**: of treatment, pain, death, the future of the family.
- **Anger**, guilt ("what did I do wrong?") or a sense of injustice.
- **Sadness**, crying, a lump in the throat.
- Physical symptoms: insomnia, loss of appetite, a racing heart, difficulty concentrating.

All of this is a **normal reaction** to a serious threat, not a sign of weakness or of a mental illness.

## How it evolves

Emotions are not a straight line. They usually ease once there is a treatment plan, and they flare again at key moments: waiting for results, starting treatment, finishing it, check-ups, a relapse. **Finishing treatment** is often unexpectedly hard: visits become less frequent, there is less contact with the team and the fear of recurrence appears.

Studies suggest that around **one in three people with cancer** has a mental health problem at some point, most often anxiety, depression or an adjustment disorder. Most people, however, adapt with time and support.

## Normal distress and a problem that needs attention

| Usually normal | Worth asking for help |
|---|---|
| Sadness and worry that come and go | Sadness or anxiety most of the day for more than two weeks |
| Bad nights around important appointments | Persistent insomnia |
| Moments of fear about the future | Fear that prevents you from going to tests or treatment |
| Needing time alone | Isolating yourself from everyone |
| Thinking about death | Thoughts of not wanting to live or of harming yourself |

If you recognise yourself in the right-hand column, see [Anxiety, depression and fear of recurrence](../anxiety-depression-and-fear-of-recurrence/).

## What usually helps

- **Accurate information**, adapted to what you want to know. Uncertainty causes more anxiety than knowing.
- **Talking** about what you feel with people you trust, without having to look strong.
- Keeping some **routines** and activities that give you pleasure.
- **Physical exercise** adapted to you.
- Limiting internet searches and checking information with your team.
- Contact with **other patients**, in groups or associations.
- Asking for psychological support early, without waiting for things to get worse: see [What is psycho-oncology](../what-is-psycho-oncology/).

## About "positive thinking"

There is no good evidence that personality or a "positive attitude" changes how cancer progresses. Demanding optimism of yourself, or of the patient, adds pressure and guilt. It is valid to feel bad, and accepting it is often the first step to feeling better.
