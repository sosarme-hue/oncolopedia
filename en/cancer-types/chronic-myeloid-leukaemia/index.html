Chronic myeloid leukaemia

The leukaemia that launched the era of targeted therapy. With imatinib and its successors, life expectancy is near normal and some people can stop treatment.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common 1-2 cases per 100,000 a year; median age 55-60
Cause Philadelphia chromosome: BCR-ABL1 fusion
Finding High white cell count on a blood test, enlarged spleen
Treatment Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (imatinib, dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib, asciminib, ponatinib)
Survival Life expectancy close to that of the general population

A medical milestone

In 1960, Peter Nowell and David Hungerford discovered in Philadelphia a small chromosome in this leukaemia's cells: the first genetic alteration linked to a cancer. In 1973 Janet Rowley showed it was a translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, creating the BCR-ABL1 fusion gene, a permanently active enzyme. In 2001, imatinib (developed with Brian Druker) became the first drug designed against a specific molecular alteration, turning a fatal disease into a chronic one.

Phases

Chronic (most at diagnosis), accelerated and blast (like an acute leukaemia). With treatment, progression is now uncommon.

Treatment and monitoring

  • Daily oral tyrosine kinase inhibitors. If one fails or is not tolerated, another is used; asciminib works differently (allosterically) and ponatinib covers the T315I mutation.
  • Monitoring with blood quantitative BCR-ABL1 PCR every 3 months: the aim is a major and deep molecular response.
  • Stopping treatment: in people with sustained deep molecular response for years, about half can stop medication without relapse (treatment-free remission), with close monitoring.
  • Transplant only for resistant or advanced-phase disease.

See also: Leukaemias, Myeloproliferative neoplasms, Targeted therapy.
