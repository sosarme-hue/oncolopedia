## Why it matters

Every week there are headlines about "the cure for cancer". Behind them may be an important advance, a preliminary result in mice or a marketing campaign. For someone living with cancer, false hope or a false alarm has a real cost.

## Ten questions

1. **Who was it tested in?** Cells, mice or people. Many treatments that work in the lab fail in humans.
2. **How many people?** Ten patients are not the same as ten thousand.
3. **What was it compared with?** A treatment with no comparison group can look effective for reasons unrelated to it.
4. **What phase of study?** Phase I and II results are preliminary; phase III results are more solid.
5. **What was measured?** A tumour shrinking is not the same as living longer or better.
6. **How much benefit?** "Cuts risk by 50%" may mean going from 2 to 1 in a thousand. Look for the absolute numbers.
7. **Where was it published?** A peer-reviewed paper, a conference abstract or a press release?
8. **Who funded or announced it?** That does not invalidate the result, but it should be considered.
9. **Which cancer?** "Cancer" is not one disease: an advance in one subtype does not apply to all.
10. **Is it available?** Years can pass between a good result and an approved, funded treatment.

## Warning signs

- It promises to cure **all cancers** or "what doctors don't want you to know".
- It relies on personal **testimonials**, not studies.
- It asks you to **abandon** conventional treatment.
- The person announcing it is selling it, often expensively and online.
- It treats "natural" as meaning "harmless".

## And if a story gives you hope

Take it to your oncologist. They will tell you whether it applies to you, whether a trial is available or why it is not an option for you.
