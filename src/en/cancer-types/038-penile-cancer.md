## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | Under 1 case per 100,000 men in Europe; up to 10 times higher in Brazil, Uganda or India |
| **Type** | Squamous cell carcinoma (95%) |
| **Risk factors** | **HPV**, **phimosis**, poor hygiene, tobacco, lichen sclerosus, HIV |
| **Sign** | A sore, ulcer, patch or lump on the glans or foreskin that does not heal |
| **Treatment** | Organ-sparing surgery, dynamic sentinel node, chemotherapy |

## Prevention

- **HPV vaccine** for boys.
- **Circumcision** in childhood reduces risk; treating phimosis.
- Hygiene, not smoking.

## Symptoms and diagnosis

Any **lesion that does not heal within four weeks** on the penis (ulcer, wart, colour change, thickening, smelly discharge under the foreskin) should be assessed by a urologist. Many men seek help late out of embarrassment. It is diagnosed by **biopsy**, and groin nodes are assessed by examination, ultrasound and **dynamic sentinel node** biopsy.

## Treatment

- **Precursor lesions** (intraepithelial neoplasia): creams (imiquimod, 5-FU), laser or surgery.
- **Organ-sparing surgery**: local excision, glansectomy with reconstruction, where possible, with good functional and cosmetic results.
- **Partial or total penectomy** for large tumours.
- **Groin nodes**: dynamic sentinel node or lymphadenectomy; paclitaxel, ifosfamide and cisplatin (TIP) chemotherapy before surgery for bulky nodes.
- Advanced disease: chemotherapy; immunotherapy in research (international InPACT trial).

## Outlook

Survival is over 85% without node involvement; node involvement is the most important factor. Because it is rare, treatment in **reference centres** is advisable (in the UK, care is centralised in supra-regional centres).
