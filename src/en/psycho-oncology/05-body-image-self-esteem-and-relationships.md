## The body changes

Cancer and its treatments can leave visible and invisible marks: **scars**, the loss of a breast or part of the body, an **ostomy**, hair loss, weight changes, skin changes, swelling (lymphoedema), early menopause. Some are temporary and others permanent. It is normal to feel strange in front of the mirror, to avoid looking at yourself or being looked at, or to feel less attractive.

These feelings do not depend on how "big" the change is from the outside, but on what it means to each person. They usually ease with time, but sometimes they persist and affect self-esteem, social life and relationships.

## What can help

- **Look at the change gradually**, at your own pace, and get to know your new body.
- **Practical solutions**: wigs, scarves, prostheses, adapted swimwear, make-up workshops, ostomy specialist nurses. Many cancer charities and hospitals offer them.
- **Reconstruction** after a mastectomy, immediate or later, or deciding not to have it: all options are valid.
- **Physiotherapy** and exercise to recover strength and confidence in the body.
- **Talking** about it with your team and with people who have been through it.
- **Psychological support** if the discomfort lasts or limits your life: see [What is psycho-oncology](../what-is-psycho-oncology/).

## Relationships

Cancer affects both partners. Roles change (one cares, the other is cared for), fears appear that sometimes are not said out loud, and sexual life may change. Some couples grow closer and others go through crises.

- **Talk openly** about what each of you feels and needs; avoid guessing.
- The partner may fear hurting or not know how to approach: say what you want.
- Find moments as a couple that are not about the illness.
- **Couples therapy** and sexual counselling help. See [Sexuality and menopause](../../living-with-cancer/sexuality-and-menopause/).

## In young people and adolescents

At these ages, appearance and fitting in with the group are especially important. Changes in body image can hit hard. Groups of young people with cancer, adapted psychological support and keeping contact with friends and school help a great deal.
