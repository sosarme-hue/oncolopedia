## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | About 400,000 cases a year worldwide (ages 0-19); about 1,900 children a year in the UK |
| **Most common** | Leukaemias (30%), nervous system tumours (20-25%), lymphomas, neuroblastoma, Wilms, sarcomas, retinoblastoma |
| **Causes** | Almost never preventable; 8-10% from inherited predisposition |
| **Cure** | Over 80% at five years in Europe; under 30% in many low-income countries |
| **Where to treat** | Paediatric and teenage oncology units |

## How it differs

- It arises from developing (embryonic) cells, not from damage accumulated over decades.
- It is almost never related to lifestyle and cannot be prevented.
- It responds better to chemotherapy, but treatment can leave **late effects** on a growing body.
- Almost all children are treated within international cooperative **trials** or protocols (SIOP, COG), which explains much of the progress.

## Warning signs

Most of these symptoms are due to common illnesses, but see a doctor if they **persist**:

- Pallor, tiredness, unexplained bruises or tiny red spots, prolonged fever.
- A **lump** in the abdomen, neck or elsewhere.
- Persistent or night-time **bone pain**, limping.
- Headache with **morning vomiting**, changes in walking or vision.
- A **white pupil** in photos (retinoblastoma).
- Weight loss, night sweats, swollen glands.

## Treatment

Chemotherapy, surgery, radiotherapy (increasingly with **protons**), transplant, immunotherapy (blinatumomab, anti-GD2 antibodies, **CAR-T**) and targeted therapy according to molecular profile. It comes with psychological, educational (hospital schools) and social support for the whole family.

## Survivors

Europe has over 500,000 childhood cancer survivors. Two in three have some **late effect** (heart, fertility, growth, hormones, learning, second cancers), so they need **long-term follow-up** with a "survivorship passport" summarising their treatment.

## Teenagers and young adults

People aged 15-25 are "between two worlds" and have less access to trials. Specific teenage and young adult units are recommended, such as those supported by Teenage Cancer Trust in the UK.
