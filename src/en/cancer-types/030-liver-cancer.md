## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | 870,000 cases a year worldwide (sixth most common, third cause of cancer death) |
| **Type** | Hepatocellular carcinoma (80-85%); intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (10-15%) |
| **Causes** | Cirrhosis from hepatitis B or C, alcohol, metabolic fatty liver (MASLD) |
| **Prevention** | Hepatitis B vaccine, hepatitis C treatment, avoiding heavy drinking, weight control |
| **Detection** | Ultrasound every 6 months in people with cirrhosis |
| **Treatment** | Resection, transplant, ablation, chemoembolisation, immunotherapy |
| **Survival** | About 20% at five years; over 70% if found early |

## What it is

**Hepatocellular carcinoma** (HCC) arises from **hepatocytes**, the main liver cells. It should not be confused with **liver metastases** from other cancers (colon, breast, pancreas), much more common in Europe and treated according to the original tumour.

In 80-90% of cases it develops on **cirrhosis**: a liver damaged over years fills with scars and regenerating nodules, where mutations accumulate.

Primary liver cancers also include intrahepatic [cholangiocarcinoma](../cholangiocarcinoma/) and, in children, [hepatoblastoma](../hepatoblastoma/).

## How common it is

It is very common in East Asia and sub-Saharan Africa because of hepatitis B and aflatoxins. In Europe and North America it is rising because of **metabolic fatty liver disease** (MASLD, formerly NAFLD) linked to obesity and diabetes, while hepatitis C is falling thanks to antivirals.

## Risk factors

| Factor | Comment |
|---|---|
| **Chronic hepatitis B** | Can cause cancer even without cirrhosis |
| **Chronic hepatitis C** | Now cured in over 95% with antivirals; risk falls but does not disappear once cirrhosis exists |
| **Alcohol** | Major cause of cirrhosis in Europe |
| **Metabolic fatty liver (MASLD/MASH)** | Linked to obesity, diabetes and metabolic syndrome; a growing cause |
| **Aflatoxins** | Fungal toxins in poorly stored foods (peanuts, maize) in hot countries |
| **Others** | Haemochromatosis, tobacco, biliary cirrhosis, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency |

## Prevention

- **Hepatitis B vaccine**, part of childhood schedules in most countries: the first vaccine against cancer.
- **Diagnosing and treating hepatitis C** (testing at least once in life) and controlling hepatitis B with antivirals.
- Limiting alcohol, keeping a healthy weight, exercising. **Coffee** is linked to lower risk.
- **Ultrasound surveillance every 6 months** (with or without alpha-fetoprotein) for people with cirrhosis or high-risk hepatitis B. It finds small tumours treatable with curative intent.

## Symptoms

Early tumours cause no symptoms, which is why surveillance is key. In advanced stages: right-sided abdominal pain, weight loss, tiredness, **decompensated cirrhosis** (ascites, jaundice, encephalopathy), a palpable mass.

## Diagnosis

- **Contrast CT or MRI**: HCC has a typical pattern (enhancing in the arterial phase and "washing out" later) that allows diagnosis **without biopsy** in a cirrhotic liver (LI-RADS system).
- Blood **alpha-fetoprotein (AFP)**.
- Biopsy if imaging is atypical or there is no cirrhosis.
- Assessment of liver function (**Child-Pugh**, MELD) and general condition.

## Stages and treatment (BCLC system)

The **Barcelona (BCLC)** classification, created at the Hospital Clínic by Jordi Bruix and Josep M. Llovet, is the world reference: it links tumour, liver function and general condition with recommended treatment.

| BCLC stage | Description | Treatment | Survival |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0 (very early)** | One nodule < 2 cm | Ablation or resection | > 5 years |
| **A (early)** | One or up to 3 nodules < 3 cm | Resection, **transplant** or ablation | > 5 years |
| **B (intermediate)** | Multinodular, no vascular invasion | **Chemoembolisation** (TACE), transplant in selected cases, systemic treatment | > 2.5 years |
| **C (advanced)** | Vascular invasion or metastases | **Immunotherapy** and other drugs | > 2 years |
| **D (terminal)** | Very poor liver function | Palliative care | 3-6 months |

**Treatments**:
- **Surgical resection**: if liver function is good and there is no significant portal hypertension.
- **Liver transplant**: cures both tumour and cirrhosis. **Milan** criteria (one nodule up to 5 cm or up to three up to 3 cm).
- **Ablation** by radiofrequency or microwave for small tumours.
- **Chemoembolisation (TACE)** and **radioembolisation** (with yttrium-90 microspheres).
- **Stereotactic radiotherapy** in selected cases.
- **Systemic treatment**: **atezolizumab with bevacizumab** (IMbrave150 trial) or **durvalumab with tremelimumab** (HIMALAYA trial) first line; also nivolumab with ipilimumab. Lenvatinib, sorafenib, regorafenib, cabozantinib and ramucirumab in other lines.

## Outlook

Overall, about 20% at five years in Europe, but over 70% for tumours found by surveillance and treated with transplant or resection. Immunotherapy has extended survival in advanced disease, with some patients alive at five years.

## After treatment

Relapses are common (up to 70% at five years after resection) because the liver remains diseased, so CT or MRI is repeated every 3-6 months. Controlling the cause (stopping alcohol, antivirals, weight) is as important as treating the tumour.

## Questions for your team

- How well is my liver working (Child-Pugh)?
- What BCLC stage am I?
- Am I a candidate for a transplant?
- What can I do to protect my liver?
