Psycho-oncology

Anxiety, depression and fear of recurrence

How to recognise anxiety and depression during and after cancer, what fear of recurrence is, and which psychological and drug treatments have been shown to help.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

Anxiety

Anxiety is a constant state of alert: worry that cannot be switched off, restlessness, muscle tension, a racing heart, difficulty breathing or sleeping. It is especially common around tests, results and the start of treatment. Some people have panic attacks or a strong fear of needles, scanners or hospitals.

Depression

Depression is more than sadness. It is suspected when, for at least two weeks, there is low mood most of the day or a loss of interest in almost everything, together with several of these: changes in sleep or appetite, feelings of guilt or worthlessness, difficulty concentrating, hopelessness, thoughts of death.

In cancer it is harder to recognise, because tiredness, loss of appetite and insomnia can also be caused by the illness or its treatment. The key clues are hopelessness, loss of interest and feelings of worthlessness. Untreated depression lowers quality of life and makes it harder to follow treatment. It can be treated.

Fear of recurrence

Fear that the cancer will come back is the most frequent concern among survivors: most have it to some degree, and in a smaller group it is intense. It shows up as constant checking of the body, interpreting any ache as a relapse, great anxiety before check-ups ("scanxiety"), or the opposite, avoiding them. There are specific therapies that reduce it.

See also: Long-term survivors.

Treatments that work

  • Psychological therapies: cognitive behavioural therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, mindfulness-based interventions, meaning-centred therapy. They are the first option for mild and moderate symptoms.
  • Medication: antidepressants and other drugs when symptoms are moderate or severe, or therapy is not enough. Some interact with cancer treatments (for example, certain antidepressants with tamoxifen), so the prescribing doctor must know your full treatment.
  • Physical exercise, which improves mood and tiredness.
  • Support groups and good communication with the medical team.

Guidelines recommend stepped care: from information and self-help to specialist therapy, depending on severity.

Get urgent help if...

  • You think about suicide or harming yourself.
  • You feel you cannot go on or that you are a burden for others.
  • You have stopped eating, drinking or taking your medicines.

Call the emergency number (112 in Europe, 999 in the UK, 911 in the US) or a crisis line: 024 in Spain, 116 123 (Samaritans) in the UK and Ireland, 988 in the US. More resources in Where to get psychological help.
