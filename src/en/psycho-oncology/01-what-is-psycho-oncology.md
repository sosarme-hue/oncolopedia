## A definition

**Psycho-oncology** is the part of cancer care that looks after the mind. It deals with two things: the **emotional, social and family impact** of cancer at every stage, and the **psychological and behavioural factors** that influence prevention, early detection, treatment and survival. It was born in the 1970s, when it became common to tell patients their diagnosis and it was clear that they needed support to face it.

Today international guidelines consider psychological care **part of good cancer care**, not an extra. Many hospitals measure **emotional distress** with simple questionnaires, as they do with pain.

## What it does

- Helps people cope with the diagnosis, treatment decisions and uncertainty.
- Treats **anxiety, depression**, insomnia and **fear of recurrence**.
- Supports adaptation to physical changes: scars, ostomies, hair loss, fatigue.
- Helps with communication within the family and with the medical team, including **how to talk to children**.
- Supports relationships and sexuality.
- Accompanies people in advanced disease and at the end of life, and the family in **grief**.
- Supports carers and, in some services, also healthcare professionals.

The most used therapies are psychoeducation, **cognitive behavioural therapy**, mindfulness-based interventions, meaning-centred therapy and support groups. When needed, they are combined with medication.

## Who provides it

- **Clinical psychologists** specialised in oncology, in psycho-oncology units or in mental health services.
- **Psychiatrists**, when medication is needed or the problem is severe.
- **Palliative care teams**, which include psychological support.
- Psychologists from **cancer charities**, often free of charge.

Oncology nurses and social workers are often the first to detect that someone needs help.

## When to ask for it

You do not need to be "very bad" to ask. It makes sense if:

- Sadness, worry or fear take up most of your day for more than two weeks.
- You cannot sleep, eat or concentrate because of what you are going through.
- You avoid appointments or tests because of anxiety.
- You feel unable to make decisions about treatment.
- There are serious tensions in the family or with your partner.
- You have thoughts of not wanting to live. In that case, **ask for help straight away**: see [Where to get psychological help](../where-to-get-psychological-help/).
