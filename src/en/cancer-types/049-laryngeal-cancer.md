## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | 190,000 cases a year worldwide; mostly men |
| **Cause** | **Tobacco** (90%) and alcohol |
| **Areas** | Glottis (vocal cords, 60-70%), supraglottis, subglottis |
| **Sign** | **Hoarseness for more than 3 weeks** |
| **Treatment** | Laser surgery or radiotherapy for early stages; chemoradiotherapy or laryngectomy for advanced |

## What it is

The **larynx** contains the vocal cords and protects the airway when swallowing. **Vocal cord** (glottic) tumours cause hoarseness very early and have almost no lymph drainage, so they are usually found at a curable stage. **Supraglottic** tumours cause fewer early symptoms and more neck metastases.

## Symptoms

Persistent hoarseness or voice change, painful swallowing, earache, cough, difficulty or noisy breathing (stridor), a neck lump.

## Diagnosis

Camera **laryngoscopy**, biopsy under anaesthetic, CT or MRI and, in advanced cases, PET-CT.

## Treatment

- **Early stages**: **transoral CO2 laser surgery** or **radiotherapy**; both cure over 90% of glottic T1 tumours. The choice depends on voice and preference.
- **Advanced**: **organ preservation** with chemoradiotherapy (Veterans Affairs trial, 1991, and RTOG 91-11), or **total laryngectomy** for tumours destroying cartilage or a larynx that no longer works.
- After laryngectomy, the person breathes through a **stoma** in the neck. Voice is restored with a **voice prosthesis** (a valve between trachea and oesophagus), oesophageal speech or an electrolarynx. Laryngectomee groups offer very valuable support.

## Outlook

Over 90% for glottic T1; 50-60% in advanced stages. Quitting smoking is essential.
