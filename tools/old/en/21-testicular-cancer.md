## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Who** | Mainly men aged 15 to 40 |
| **Types** | Germ cell tumours: seminomas and non-seminomas |
| **Sign** | A lump or hardness in a testicle, usually painless |
| **Markers** | Alpha-fetoprotein, beta-hCG and LDH in the blood |
| **Cure** | Over 95% overall, and about 80% even with metastases |

## What it is

Almost all testicular cancers arise from **germ cells**, which give rise to sperm. They are divided into **seminomas**, which grow more slowly and are very sensitive to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and **non-seminomas** (embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumour, choriocarcinoma, teratoma), often mixed.

## Risk factors

The main one is an **undescended testicle** (cryptorchidism) in childhood, even if corrected. Also a previous cancer in the other testicle, or in a father or brother.

## Symptoms

A **lump** or hard area in a testicle, often painless, a feeling of heaviness in the scrotum or swelling. Less often, back pain or breast enlargement from hormones made by the tumour. Knowing how your testicles normally feel helps you notice changes.

## Diagnosis

Scrotal **ultrasound** and blood **tumour markers**, which here really are useful for diagnosis, outlook and follow-up. Unlike other cancers, no biopsy is taken through the scrotum: the testicle is removed through the groin (**inguinal orchidectomy**), which confirms the diagnosis. Staging is by CT.

## Treatment

- **Orchidectomy**, which does not affect sexual function if the other testicle is normal. A prosthesis can be fitted.
- At early stages, often just close **surveillance**, or a short course of chemotherapy.
- With metastases, **cisplatin-based chemotherapy** (such as BEP), which in the late 1970s made this one of the first solid tumours curable at an advanced stage.
- **Sperm banking** should be offered before treatment.

## Afterwards

Follow-up lasts years, with markers and imaging. Survivors may have late effects of chemotherapy, such as cardiovascular risk or hearing problems, which are monitored.
