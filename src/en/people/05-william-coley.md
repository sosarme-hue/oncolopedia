## A patient who changed him

In 1890 the young surgeon William Coley treated Elizabeth Dashiell, a 17-year-old with a sarcoma in her hand. Despite amputation of her arm, she died within weeks. Coley was so affected that he searched the hospital records for sarcoma cases that had done better.

## An infection that cured

He found the case of a German immigrant with a sarcoma in the neck who, after a severe **erysipelas** (a streptococcal skin infection), had been cured. Coley traced him years later, healthy. He thought the infection had roused the body against the tumour.

## Coley's toxins

From 1891 he injected live streptococci into tumours, and later a mixture of killed bacteria (streptococcus and *Serratia marcescens*), **Coley's toxins**, which caused high fever. He described regressions in some sarcomas and lymphomas. Results were inconsistent and hard to reproduce, and with the arrival of radiotherapy and chemotherapy the method was forgotten.

## A pioneer recognised late

We now know that the immune system can recognise and destroy tumour cells. Modern **immunotherapy**, with checkpoint inhibitors or CAR-T cells, does in a targeted way what Coley attempted blindly. His daughter, Helen Coley Nauts, compiled his cases and founded the Cancer Research Institute in 1953.
