## A perfect target

**Chronic myeloid leukaemia** has a single, known cause: the **Philadelphia chromosome**, discovered in 1960, which creates the ***BCR-ABL*** fusion gene. Its protein is a tyrosine kinase that is always switched on, telling cells to multiply.

In the 1990s chemists at Ciba-Geigy (later Novartis), including Nicholas Lydon, designed molecules able to block kinases. Brian Druker, an oncologist in Portland, Oregon, showed in the laboratory that one of them, STI571, killed leukaemia cells without harming normal ones, and pushed to get it to patients.

## The trial

In the first trial, published in 2001, virtually all patients given adequate doses had their blood counts return to normal. The drug, named **imatinib**, was approved in record time. Before, half of patients died within about five years; with imatinib and its successors, most have a life expectancy close to that of the general population.

## The model for precision medicine

Imatinib showed that a treatment could be designed against the molecular change driving a cancer. It became the model for **targeted therapies**. It is also effective in **gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GIST)**, which have mutations in another kinase, *KIT*.

Druker shared the 2009 Lasker Award with Nicholas Lydon and Charles Sawyers.
