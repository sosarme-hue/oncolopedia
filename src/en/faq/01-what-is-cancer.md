## Cells that lose control

The body is made of trillions of cells that divide, do their job and die in an orderly way, following the instructions in their genes. **Cancer** appears when a cell builds up **changes in its DNA** that make it divide out of control, ignore signals to stop or die, and escape the body's defences.

These changes mainly affect two kinds of genes: **oncogenes**, which act like a stuck accelerator, and **tumour suppressor genes**, brakes that stop working. They usually build up over years, through chance errors in DNA copying, age and exposures such as tobacco or ultraviolet light. That is why cancer is more common in older people.

## Benign and malignant

A **tumour** is a mass of abnormal cells. It is **benign** if it grows without invading surrounding tissue or spreading, like most moles or fibroids. It is **malignant**, that is, a cancer, if it can **invade** nearby tissue and form **metastases**: cells that break away, travel in the blood or lymph and grow in other organs.

Some cancers, such as leukaemias, do not form a lump, because they start in blood cells.

## Not a single disease

There are more than two hundred types of cancer, named after the cell they start from: **carcinomas** (from linings, the most common), **sarcomas** (from supporting tissues), **leukaemias**, **lymphomas** and **myelomas** (from the blood and immune system), nervous system tumours and others. Even within one organ there are subtypes that behave and are treated very differently. That is why figures and treatments for one cancer do not apply to another.

## The abilities of cancer

The researchers Douglas Hanahan and Robert Weinberg summed up the properties cancer cells acquire: multiplying without a signal, ignoring brakes, avoiding cell death, dividing indefinitely, forming blood vessels, invading and spreading, changing their metabolism and escaping the immune system, among others. Many current treatments target one of them.
