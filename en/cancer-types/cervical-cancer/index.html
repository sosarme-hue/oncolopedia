Cancer types

Cervical cancer

A cancer caused almost always by the human papillomavirus, and therefore one of the most preventable. Vaccination and screening make elimination a realistic goal.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common About 660,000 cases and 350,000 deaths worldwide in 2022, mostly in low-income countries
Cause Persistent infection with high-risk HPV types, in over 95% of cases
Prevention HPV vaccine and screening with cytology or HPV testing
Signs Vaginal bleeding between periods, after sex or after menopause
WHO goal Eliminate it as a public health problem this century

What it is

The cervix is the part of the womb that opens into the vagina. Cancer develops after a persistent infection with human papillomavirus (HPV), a very common sexually transmitted infection that almost always clears on its own. When it persists, it can cause precancerous changes (called CIN or high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions) over many years, which can become cancer if untreated.

That long pre-cancer phase is what makes screening so effective: abnormal cells are found and treated before cancer develops.

Prevention

  • HPV vaccine: offered to girls and boys around ages 11-13 in many countries (age 12 in Spain), with catch-up programmes. It works best before sexual activity begins.
  • Screening: HPV testing every five years from around 30-35 to 65 is replacing cytology in most of Europe; cytology every three years is used at younger ages. Vaccinated women still need screening.
  • Smoking increases the chance that the infection persists.

Symptoms

Precancerous changes cause no symptoms. Cancer can cause vaginal bleeding outside periods, after sex or after menopause, abnormal discharge and pelvic pain.

Diagnosis and treatment

An abnormal screening result leads to colposcopy, in which the cervix is examined under magnification and biopsied. Precancerous changes are treated with a small cone biopsy or excision that removes the affected area.

Cancer is treated according to stage: surgery for small tumours (sometimes preserving fertility), and chemoradiotherapy with brachytherapy for larger ones. Immunotherapy is added in advanced disease.

A strategy for elimination

In 2020 WHO launched the 90-70-90 strategy for 2030: vaccinate 90% of girls, screen 70% of women and treat 90% of those who need it. Australia and other countries are approaching elimination.
