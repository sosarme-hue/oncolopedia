## What it is

**Targeted therapies** act on a specific molecule the tumour needs to grow: a mutated protein, an excess protein or a signalling pathway. Unlike chemotherapy, which attacks any fast-dividing cell, they go after a specific weak spot in the cancer. They almost always require a **biomarker** showing the tumour has that target.

The model was **imatinib**, approved in 2001 for chronic myeloid leukaemia: a daily tablet that blocks the BCR-ABL protein and controls the disease for years.

## Main types

- **Tyrosine kinase inhibitors** (names ending in "-tinib"): tablets that block enzymes that pass on growth signals. Examples: osimertinib (*EGFR*), alectinib or lorlatinib (*ALK*), dabrafenib (*BRAF*), imatinib (*BCR-ABL*, *KIT*).
- **Monoclonal antibodies** (ending in "-mab"): bind to a protein on the cell surface. Examples: trastuzumab (HER2), cetuximab (EGFR), rituximab (CD20).
- **Antibody-drug conjugates** (ADCs): antibodies that carry a very potent drug to the target cell, such as trastuzumab deruxtecan or enfortumab vedotin.
- **Anti-angiogenics**: block the formation of the tumour's blood vessels, such as bevacizumab.
- **PARP inhibitors**: exploit the fact that some tumours (for example with *BRCA* changes) cannot repair their DNA properly.
- **CDK4/6, mTOR, BTK and BCL-2 inhibitors** and others.

## Strengths and limits

- They can achieve **fast, deep responses**, often with good quality of life and as tablets.
- They have **their own side effects**: skin problems, diarrhoea, high blood pressure, liver or heart changes, depending on the drug. They need monitoring.
- Tumours usually develop **resistance** over time, as cells find another pathway or acquire new mutations. That is why next-generation drugs and combinations are being developed, and sometimes a repeat biopsy or a liquid biopsy is done.
- Having a change in the tumour **does not guarantee** an effective drug exists or is approved for that cancer.
