Living with cancer

Sexuality and menopause

Cancer and its treatments can change desire, sexual response and body image, and bring on menopause early. What is common, what can be treated and how to talk about it.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

A common topic that is rarely discussed

More than half of people treated for cancer notice changes in their sex life. Many do not mention it because they feel embarrassed or think it is not important, and many professionals do not ask. It is a legitimate concern at any age, with or without a partner, and much of it can be treated. You can raise it with your oncologist, your nurse or your GP.

Why it happens

  • Treatments: surgery on the pelvis, breast or genitals; pelvic radiotherapy; chemotherapy; hormone therapy. See Hormone therapy.
  • Physical changes: tiredness, pain, scars, ostomies, hair loss.
  • Emotional changes: anxiety, low mood, a different body image. See Body image, self-esteem and relationships.

In women

  • Early menopause: chemotherapy or treatment of the ovaries may bring it on, with hot flushes, vaginal dryness, insomnia and loss of bone mass. Discuss fertility before starting treatment: see Fertility and cancer.
  • Vaginal dryness and pain: water-based or silicone lubricants and vaginal moisturisers. Low-dose vaginal oestrogen is an option in many cases, but in hormone-sensitive breast cancer it should be decided with the oncologist.
  • Hot flushes: if hormone therapy is not possible, there are non-hormonal medicines and cognitive behavioural therapy.
  • After pelvic radiotherapy, vaginal dilators help prevent narrowing.
  • Pelvic floor physiotherapy helps with pain and incontinence.

In men

  • Erectile dysfunction after prostate or rectal surgery or pelvic radiotherapy: tablets (sildenafil, tadalafil), injections, vacuum devices or implants. Starting rehabilitation early improves results.
  • Hormone therapy for prostate cancer reduces desire and causes hot flushes and loss of muscle; exercise helps.
  • Changes in ejaculation after surgery.

For everyone

  • Talking with your partner about what you feel and what you need reduces the pressure.
  • Intimacy is not only penetration: touch, closeness and other ways of giving pleasure count.
  • Sexual counselling and couples therapy work. Ask if your hospital has a sexology or psycho-oncology service.
  • Ask about contraception during treatment: some drugs can harm a pregnancy.
