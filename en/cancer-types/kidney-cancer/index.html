Cancer types

Kidney cancer

More than half are found by chance on an ultrasound or CT scan. Small tumours are often treated by removing only part of the kidney, and in advanced disease immunotherapy has lengthened survival for many patients.

At a glance
Frequency About 435,000 cases a year worldwide (GLOBOCAN 2022) and about 8,000 in Spain
Causes Smoking, obesity, high blood pressure, chronic kidney disease, inherited syndromes such as von Hippel-Lindau
How it is found Often by chance on a scan; sometimes blood in the urine, pain in the side or a lump
Diagnosis Contrast CT; biopsy if it will change the decision
Treatment Partial or radical nephrectomy; surveillance or ablation for small masses; immunotherapy alone or with anti-angiogenic drugs if advanced

What it is

The most common kidney cancer in adults is renal cell carcinoma, which starts in the kidney tubules. It has several types:

  • Clear cell (about 75%), linked to loss of the VHL gene. It has its own page.
  • Papillary (about 15%).
  • Chromophobe (about 5%), with a good outlook.
  • Rare, often more aggressive types: collecting duct, medullary (in people with sickle cell trait), TFE3 or TFEB translocation, or fumarate hydratase (FH) deficient.

Tumours of the renal pelvis are not renal cell carcinomas but urothelial, like bladder cancer: see upper tract urothelial cancer. In children the typical kidney cancer is Wilms tumour. To compare with the bladder, see Bladder and kidney cancer.

How common it is

It is twice as common in men as in women, mostly between 60 and 70. It has risen partly because more ultrasound and CT scans are done for other reasons.

Risk factors

  • Smoking.
  • Obesity and high blood pressure.
  • Chronic kidney disease, especially on dialysis.
  • Inherited syndromes: von Hippel-Lindau, Birt-Hogg-Dubé, hereditary leiomyomatosis (FH), tuberous sclerosis, BAP1. Worth testing for if the cancer appears young, in both kidneys, as several tumours or with a family history.
  • Workplace exposure to trichloroethylene.

Symptoms

Today more than half are found by chance, without symptoms. The classic triad (blood in the urine, pain in the side and a lump) is rare and late. Other possible signs are fever without infection, tiredness, anaemia or, on the contrary, too many red blood cells, high calcium or a varicocele that appears suddenly.

Diagnosis

  1. Abdominal contrast CT (or MRI), which is usually enough to diagnose it.
  2. Chest CT for staging.
  3. Needle biopsy of small masses when the result will change the decision, or before systemic treatment.
  4. Kidney function tests, which guide the choice of surgery.

Treatment

Small masses (under 4 cm): - Partial nephrectomy, which removes only the tumour and preserves kidney function. - Active surveillance in older people or those with other illnesses, because many grow very slowly. - Ablation (cryotherapy or radiofrequency) in selected cases.

Larger or locally advanced tumours: - Radical nephrectomy, often laparoscopic or robotic. - Pembrolizumab for a year after surgery at high risk of relapse: in the KEYNOTE-564 trial it reduced relapse and lengthened survival.

Advanced disease: - Combinations of immunotherapy (nivolumab with ipilimumab) or of immunotherapy with anti-angiogenic drugs (pembrolizumab with axitinib or lenvatinib, nivolumab with cabozantinib), chosen by risk and type. - Later: cabozantinib, belzutifan (a HIF-2α inhibitor), tivozanib and others. - Surveillance without treatment for a time in some patients with little disease and slow growth. - Conventional chemotherapy is of little use; stereotactic radiotherapy and surgery for isolated metastases help in selected cases.

Outlook

Small, localised tumours are cured in the great majority of cases. The risk of relapse depends on size, grade and whether the tumour invades the fat, veins or lymph nodes. In metastatic disease, immunotherapy combinations have lengthened survival and some patients have very durable responses, although not everyone responds.

After treatment

After one kidney is removed, the other is usually enough. It is worth protecting kidney function: controlling blood pressure and blood sugar, avoiding anti-inflammatory painkillers without medical advice and drinking enough fluids. CT follow-up lasts years, because relapses can be late.

Questions for your team

  • What type of kidney cancer is it and what grade?
  • Can you remove just the tumour and keep the kidney?
  • Is it reasonable to watch it instead of operating?
  • Is my relapse risk high enough to justify treatment after surgery?
  • Should I have genetic testing?
