## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | Breast cancer with excess HER2 protein or amplification of its gene, *ERBB2* |
| **How common** | About 15-20% of breast cancers |
| **How it is measured** | Immunohistochemistry (3+ is positive) and, if borderline (2+), in situ hybridisation |
| **Treatment** | Chemotherapy with trastuzumab and pertuzumab, surgery, radiotherapy; antibody-drug conjugates |
| **Outlook** | Over 90% long-term survival in treated early disease |

## What it is

**HER2** is a receptor on the cell surface that passes on growth signals. In this subtype the gene that makes it is **amplified**: there are many copies, and the cell has hundreds of times more receptors than normal. This makes the tumour grow quickly. Before the 1990s, HER2-positive breast cancer had one of the worst outlooks.

The arrival of **trastuzumab**, a monoclonal antibody against HER2 approved in 1998 for metastatic disease and in 2005-2006 after surgery, completely changed its story. It is now one of the subtypes with the best results.

It may be hormone receptor-positive (luminal HER2-positive) or negative (HER2-enriched), which also affects treatment.

## Diagnosis

It is tested on the biopsy:

- **Immunohistochemistry**: 0, 1+, 2+ or 3+. 3+ is positive; 2+ is borderline.
- **In situ hybridisation** (FISH or similar) for borderline cases: confirms whether there is amplification.

Tumours scoring 1+ or 2+ without amplification are called **HER2-low**: they are not HER2-positive, but may respond to trastuzumab deruxtecan in metastatic disease.

## Treating early disease

- Small tumours without nodes: surgery followed by **weekly paclitaxel with trastuzumab** for a year (the APT regimen), with very good results.
- Tumours over 2 cm or with nodes: **neoadjuvant treatment** with chemotherapy plus **trastuzumab and pertuzumab** (dual blockade), then surgery.
- If no tumour remains after this, a year of anti-HER2 treatment is completed.
- If **tumour remains**, treatment switches to **trastuzumab emtansine** (T-DM1), which in the KATHERINE trial halved the risk of relapse.
- **Radiotherapy** depending on surgery.
- **Hormone therapy** if hormone receptors are also positive.
- In some high-risk cases, neratinib after the year of trastuzumab.

## Metastatic disease

- First line: **trastuzumab + pertuzumab + a taxane**. In the CLEOPATRA trial median survival exceeded five years.
- Second line: **trastuzumab deruxtecan** (T-DXd), a very active antibody-drug conjugate, even in brain metastases.
- Later: **tucatinib** with trastuzumab and capecitabine, T-DM1 and others.
- **Brain metastases** are more common than in other subtypes and are treated with radiosurgery, surgery or drugs that cross the blood-brain barrier.

## Effects to watch

Anti-HER2 drugs can affect **heart function**, so an echocardiogram is done before and every few months. It is usually reversible when the drug is stopped. Trastuzumab deruxtecan can cause **pneumonitis**, lung inflammation: report any cough or breathlessness.

## Outlook

With current treatment, more than 90% of patients with early disease remain disease-free in the long term. In metastatic disease many patients live for years, and some with complete responses stay in control for a long time.

## Questions for your team

- Is my tumour also hormone receptor-positive?
- Will I have treatment before or after surgery?
- How will my heart be monitored?
- How many anti-HER2 treatments will I need, and can they be given under the skin?
