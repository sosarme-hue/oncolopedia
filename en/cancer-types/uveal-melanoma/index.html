Cancer types

Uveal melanoma

The most common primary eye cancer in adults. Very different from skin melanoma: it is not caused by the sun, is often treated while keeping the eye and tends to spread to the liver.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common 5-7 cases per million people a year in Europe
Where Choroid (90%), ciliary body, iris
Who Adults aged 50-70, light-coloured eyes
Genetics GNAQ/GNA11 mutations; loss of BAP1 and monosomy 3 indicate high risk
Treatment Plaque brachytherapy or protons; enucleation for large tumours; tebentafusp for metastases

What it is

The uvea is the pigmented layer of the eye (iris, ciliary body and choroid). Uveal melanoma arises from its melanocytes. It often causes no symptoms and is found during an eye examination; other times it causes blurred vision, flashes, shadows or visual field loss.

Diagnosis

Fundus examination by a specialist ophthalmologist, ocular ultrasound, angiography and optical coherence tomography. Biopsy is rarely needed for diagnosis, but genetic testing of the tumour (monosomy 3, BAP1, expression profile) predicts the risk of metastasis.

Eye treatment

  • Plaque brachytherapy: a plate containing iodine-125 or ruthenium-106 is stitched onto the eye for a few days. It keeps the eye and often some vision.
  • Proton therapy for tumours near the optic nerve.
  • Enucleation (removal of the eye) for very large tumours, followed by an ocular prosthesis. The COMS study showed survival is the same with brachytherapy or enucleation for medium tumours.

Metastases

Up to half of patients develop metastases, almost always in the liver, sometimes many years later. Surveillance uses liver ultrasound or MRI. Treatments: tebentafusp (the first drug to improve survival in this tumour, for HLA-A*02:01 patients), isolated hepatic perfusion with melphalan, surgery or radiotherapy for metastases, and immunotherapy.

See also: Melanoma, Retinoblastoma.
