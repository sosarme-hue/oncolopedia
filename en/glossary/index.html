Oncology glossary

57 oncology terms explained briefly and clearly, from adjuvant to TNM.

ABCFGHILMNOPRST

A

Adjuvant
Treatment given after the main treatment, usually surgery, to reduce the risk of relapse. It may be chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormone therapy or immunotherapy.
Angiogenesis
The formation of new blood vessels. Tumours stimulate it to get oxygen and nutrients, and some drugs block it.
Antibody-drug conjugate
An antibody linked to a very potent drug, which it delivers directly to cells carrying its target. Also called an ADC.

B

Benign
A tumour that does not invade nearby tissue or spread. It can cause problems through its size or location, but it is not a cancer.
Biomarker
A measurable feature that tells us about a tumour: for example a hormone receptor, HER2, a mutation or PD-L1. It helps choose treatment.
Biopsy
Taking a tissue sample to examine under the microscope. It is the test that confirms the diagnosis of most cancers.
Brachytherapy
Radiotherapy in which the radioactive source is placed inside or very close to the tumour. Used, for example, in cervical or prostate cancer.
BRCA1 and BRCA2
Genes that help repair DNA. Inherited variants that inactivate them greatly increase the risk of breast, ovarian, prostate and pancreatic cancer.

C

CAR-T
A treatment in which a patient's T cells are collected, modified in the laboratory to recognise cancer cells and infused back.
Carcinogen
An agent that can cause cancer, such as tobacco smoke, asbestos or ultraviolet radiation. IARC classifies them by the strength of the evidence.
Carcinoma
Cancer arising from epithelial cells, which line the skin, organs and glands. It is the most common type of cancer.
Carcinoma in situ
A lesion with malignant cells that have not yet broken through the layer where they started. It does not spread, but may progress if untreated.
Checkpoint inhibitor
A drug that blocks the immune system's "brakes", such as PD-1, PD-L1 or CTLA-4, so that lymphocytes attack the tumour.
Chemotherapy
Treatment with drugs that destroy fast-dividing cells. It can cure, reduce the risk of relapse or control the disease.
Clinical trial
A study in people comparing a new treatment with the usual treatment to find out whether it is effective and safe. It has phases and eligibility criteria.

F

Five-year survival
The percentage of people in a group still alive five years after diagnosis. It is a statistical measure, not a deadline.

G

Germline
Describes a genetic variant present in every cell of the body from birth. It can be inherited. The opposite of somatic.
Grade
A description of how different tumour cells look from normal cells under the microscope. A high grade usually means faster growth.

H

HER2
A protein that, when present in excess in breast or stomach tumour cells, drives their growth. There are specific drugs against it.
Hormone therapy
Treatment that blocks or reduces the hormones that drive some cancers, mainly breast and prostate cancer.
HPV
Human papillomavirus. Some types cause almost all cervical cancers and some anal, penile, vulvar and oropharyngeal cancers. There is a vaccine.

I

Immunohistochemistry
A laboratory technique that stains specific proteins in a tissue sample. It helps identify a tumour's type and which treatments may work.
Immunotherapy
Treatment that helps the immune system recognise and destroy cancer cells.

L

Leukaemia
Cancer of the blood-forming cells in the bone marrow. It can be acute or chronic, and lymphoid or myeloid.
Liquid biopsy
A test of blood or other fluids looking for fragments of tumour DNA or other signs of cancer. It has specific uses, and others still under research.
Lymphoma
Cancer of lymphocytes that usually affects the lymph nodes. Divided into Hodgkin lymphoma and non-Hodgkin lymphomas.
Lynch syndrome
An inherited predisposition to colorectal, endometrial and other cancers, caused by variants in DNA repair genes.

M

Malignant
A tumour able to invade nearby tissue and spread to other organs. A synonym for cancer.
Median survival
The time at which half the people in a study are still alive. The other half live longer. It does not predict what will happen to one person.
Metastasis
A tumour formed by cells that have broken away from the original tumour and travelled to another organ in the blood or lymph.
Microsatellite instability
A sign that the tumour cell does not correct DNA copying errors properly. Linked to Lynch syndrome and predicts a good response to immunotherapy.
Monoclonal antibody
A laboratory-made protein that recognises a specific target, for example a protein on the surface of tumour cells. Names end in "-mab".
Multiple myeloma
Cancer of the plasma cells in the bone marrow, which make antibodies. It can affect the bones, kidneys and blood.

N

Neoadjuvant
Treatment given before the main treatment, usually surgery, to shrink the tumour and see how it responds.
Neoplasm
Abnormal, uncontrolled growth of cells. It can be benign or malignant. "Malignant neoplasm" means cancer.
Net survival
Survival attributable to cancer, removing deaths from other causes. It is used to compare countries and periods.
Neutropenia
A fall in neutrophils, white blood cells that fight infection. A common effect of chemotherapy. Fever with neutropenia is an emergency.

O

Oncogene
A gene that, when altered, pushes the cell to divide out of control. It works like a stuck accelerator.
Oncology
The medical specialty that deals with cancer. It includes medical, radiation, surgical and paediatric oncology and haemato-oncology.

P

Palliative care
Care that relieves symptoms and suffering and supports the family at any stage of a serious illness. It is not limited to the last days.
Pathology
The medical specialty that studies tissues and cells under the microscope. The pathologist's report confirms whether there is cancer and what type.
PD-L1
A protein some tumour cells use to put the brakes on lymphocytes. Measuring it helps decide on some immunotherapies.
Prognosis
The expected course of a disease. In cancer it depends on the type, stage, tumour biology, the person and the response to treatment.

R

Radiotherapy
Treatment that uses ionising radiation to destroy cancer cells in a specific area of the body.
Relapse
The return of a cancer after a period in which it could not be detected. Also called recurrence.
Remission
A decrease in or disappearance of signs of cancer. Complete remission means no disease is detected with available tests.

S

Sarcoma
Cancer arising in bone or soft tissues, such as muscle, fat or blood vessels. It is uncommon.
Screening
A programme offering a test to people without symptoms to find a cancer or precancerous change in time. Breast, bowel and cervical screening are the most common.
Second opinion
A review of the diagnosis or treatment by another specialist or centre. It is a patient's right and should not offend the team.
Somatic
Describes a genetic change that appears in a cell during life, for example in a tumour. It is not inherited.
Stage
A description of how far a cancer has spread: its size, whether it affects lymph nodes and whether it has reached other organs. Usually given from 0 to IV.
Stem cell transplant
An infusion of blood stem cells, the patient's own or from a donor, after intensive chemotherapy. Often called a bone marrow transplant.

T

Targeted therapy
A drug that acts on a specific change in tumour cells, such as a mutation or an excess protein. It usually requires a biomarker.
TNM
A system for describing the stage of many solid tumours: T is the size or extent of the tumour, N the lymph nodes affected and M metastases.
Tumour
A mass of abnormal tissue. It can be benign or malignant. Not all tumours are cancers.
Tumour marker
A substance measured in the blood that can be raised in some cancers, such as PSA or CEA. It almost never diagnoses cancer on its own.
Tumour suppressor
A gene that slows cell division or repairs damage. When it is lost, the cell loses a brake. Examples: TP53, RB1, BRCA1.
