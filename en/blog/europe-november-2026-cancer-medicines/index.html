What Europe recommended in November: new medicines for lung, prostate and kidney cancer

The European Medicines Agency's medicines committee backed two new lung cancer drugs and new uses in prostate and kidney cancer. What it means, and what it does not.

Every month, the European Medicines Agency's (EMA) Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP) meets to review applications for new medicines and for new uses of medicines already approved. At its meeting of 9 to 12 November 2026 it recommended seven new medicines and extended the indications of eleven more. Several are for cancer.

First, it helps to remember what a "positive opinion" from the CHMP means: it is a recommendation, not the final authorisation. The European Commission then takes the decision, usually within about two months. And once a medicine is authorised in Europe, each country decides whether and how to fund it. That is why months can pass between the headline and the drug actually reaching hospitals.

Lung cancer: two new medicines

Non-small-cell lung cancer is where more new drugs have arrived in recent years than anywhere else, and this month adds two:

  • Sintilimab (Zybrivo). An antibody that blocks PD-1, one of the immune system's "brakes", from the same family as other checkpoint inhibitors. The recommendation is for adults with non-squamous non-small-cell lung cancer, as first-line treatment combined with chemotherapy (pemetrexed and a platinum drug). It was developed mainly in China, and it widens the choice within a group of drugs already widely used.
  • Aumolertinib (Aumseqa). An EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor, a targeted therapy for lung tumours with a mutation in that gene. These mutations are more common in people who have never smoked and in women, which is why molecular testing of the tumour at diagnosis matters so much.

Prostate cancer: a PARP inhibitor at an earlier stage

The CHMP recommended extending the use of talazoparib (Talzenna), combined with enzalutamide and standard hormone suppression, to men with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer whose tumour carries mutations in homologous recombination repair genes such as BRCA2. Until now these drugs were mostly used in castration-resistant disease, a later stage. It is an example of how precision medicine is moving treatments earlier.

Kidney cancer: belzutifan with lenvatinib

The committee also backed belzutifan (Welireg) combined with lenvatinib for adults with advanced clear cell kidney cancer that has progressed after immunotherapy. Belzutifan blocks HIF-2α, a protein that builds up in this tumour when the VHL gene fails, and it is one of the few drugs with a genuinely new mechanism in this cancer.

A sarcoma drug intended for outside Europe

A less publicised recommendation is worth noting: catequentinib (Qezzaqar) for two types of soft tissue sarcoma. It was assessed under the EU-M4all procedure, through which the EMA, working with the WHO, reviews medicines intended for countries outside the European Union, often with fewer resources. It does not mean the drug will be marketed in Europe, but it helps other countries authorise it on the basis of a rigorous assessment.

How to read this kind of news

When you read that "Europe approves a new cancer drug", ask three questions, which we expand on in How to read cancer news:

  1. For whom, exactly? The indication is almost always very specific: one tumour type, one stage and sometimes one genetic change.
  2. What benefit has it shown? Delaying progression is not the same as extending life, and the product information spells this out.
  3. When will it be available here? The CHMP opinion is only the first step.

If you have one of these cancers, the right person to tell you whether any of these drugs could help is your oncologist, who knows your case. You can read how cancer drugs are developed in New cancer drugs.
