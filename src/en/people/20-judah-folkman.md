## An observation in the Navy

While working for the US Navy in the 1960s, Judah Folkman grew tumours in isolated organs. He noticed that tumours stopped growing once they reached a few millimetres, and that they did not form blood vessels in those organs.

## Starving the tumour

In 1971 he published his hypothesis in *The New England Journal of Medicine*: tumours need to form **new blood vessels (angiogenesis)** to grow beyond a small size, and blocking that process could be a treatment. The idea was met with scepticism for years; many thought the vessels were simply an inflammatory reaction.

## From hypothesis to drug

His laboratory at Harvard identified substances that stimulate and inhibit angiogenesis. In 2004 **bevacizumab**, an antibody against vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), was approved for colorectal cancer. Anti-angiogenics are now used in colon, kidney, liver, ovarian and other cancers, and also in eye diseases such as macular degeneration.

They did not turn out to be the universal cure some predicted, but they confirmed that the tumour's surroundings are a treatment target.
