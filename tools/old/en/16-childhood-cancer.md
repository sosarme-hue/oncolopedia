## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | Rare: around 400,000 children and teenagers worldwide each year, according to WHO estimates |
| **Most common** | Leukaemias, brain and spinal cord tumours, and lymphomas |
| **Difference from adults** | Not linked to lifestyle; many tumours are embryonal |
| **Survival** | Over 80% at five years in high-income countries |
| **Where treated** | In paediatric oncology units, ideally within cooperative trials or protocols |

## What is different

Childhood cancer is not a small version of adult cancer. Breast, lung or bowel carcinomas are exceptional in children. The main types are:

- **Leukaemias**, mainly **acute lymphoblastic leukaemia**, about a third of cases.
- **Central nervous system tumours**, such as **medulloblastoma** or gliomas.
- **Lymphomas**.
- **Embryonal tumours**, arising in developing tissues: **neuroblastoma**, **Wilms tumour** (kidney), **retinoblastoma** (eye), **hepatoblastoma**.
- **Sarcomas** of bone (osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma) and soft tissue (rhabdomyosarcoma), more common in teenagers.

In most cases there is **no avoidable cause**. Parents did nothing to cause it. Some cases are linked to genetic predisposition.

## Warning signs

They are often non-specific and most are due to minor illnesses, but see a doctor if they persist:

- Pallor, tiredness, unexplained bruising or bleeding, prolonged fever.
- Persistent bone pain, especially at night, or limping.
- Morning headaches with vomiting, changes in behaviour or balance.
- A lump in the abdomen, neck or elsewhere.
- A **white reflection in the pupil** in flash photos (possible retinoblastoma).

## Treatment

Chemotherapy, surgery and radiotherapy are combined according to the tumour, with growing use of targeted therapy and immunotherapy (for example CAR-T in lymphoblastic leukaemia). Children are treated in **paediatric oncology units** using international cooperative protocols, one reason for the huge improvement in survival since the 1960s.

## After cancer

Survivors may have late effects of treatment on growth, fertility, the heart or learning, so they need long-term follow-up into adulthood. Psychological and school support, and care for siblings, are part of treatment.
