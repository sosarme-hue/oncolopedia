Cancer types

Thyroid cancer

One of the cancers with the best outlook. Its rise in recent decades is largely due to overdiagnosis of small tumours that would never have caused harm.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common About 820,000 cases worldwide in 2022, three times more in women
Types Papillary (the most common), follicular, medullary and anaplastic
Sign A lump in the neck; the vast majority of thyroid nodules are benign
Survival Over 95% at five years for papillary and follicular
Overdiagnosis Many harmless microcarcinomas have been found on scans done for other reasons

What it is

The thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland in the front of the neck that makes hormones regulating metabolism. Cancers arising in it are of several types:

  • Papillary: about 80-85%. Slow-growing with an excellent outlook, even when it spreads to neck lymph nodes.
  • Follicular: also with a good outlook.
  • Medullary: arises from other cells (C cells), produces calcitonin and in a quarter of cases is inherited through variants in the RET gene.
  • Anaplastic: very rare and very aggressive, in older people.

Risk factors

Radiation in childhood (after the Chernobyl accident, cases rose sharply among children in the area), family history and some inherited syndromes.

The overdiagnosis problem

In South Korea, when thyroid ultrasound became popular in health checks, diagnoses rose fifteen-fold in twenty years, but deaths did not change. Tiny tumours that would never have caused harm were being found. That is why looking for thyroid cancer in people without symptoms is not recommended, and small nodules that look benign are not biopsied.

Diagnosis

Ultrasound of the nodule, classified by its appearance, and fine needle aspiration when indicated. Thyroid hormone tests.

Treatment

  • Surgery: removal of half the gland (lobectomy) or all of it (thyroidectomy), depending on size and extent. After total removal, thyroid hormone is taken for life.
  • Radioactive iodine in some cases after surgery.
  • For low-risk papillary microcarcinomas, active surveillance with ultrasound may be offered instead of surgery.
  • In advanced disease that does not respond to iodine, and in medullary and anaplastic cancer, targeted therapy according to the changes found (RET, BRAF, NTRK).
