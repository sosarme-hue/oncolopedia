## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | About 1% of all breast cancers |
| **Age** | Mostly between 60 and 70 |
| **Typical sign** | A painless lump behind the nipple |
| **Inheritance** | 10-15% have an inherited variant, mainly in *BRCA2* |
| **Biology** | Over 90% hormone receptor-positive |

## What it is

Men also have a small amount of breast tissue behind the nipple, where cancer can develop. It is almost always **invasive carcinoma of no special type** that is **oestrogen receptor-positive**. Because it is little known, many men seek help late, and the share of cases with affected lymph nodes at diagnosis is higher than in women.

## Risk factors

- **Variants in *BRCA2*** (lifetime risk can reach 6-8%), and to a lesser extent *BRCA1*, *PALB2* and *CHEK2*.
- **Family history** of breast or ovarian cancer.
- **Excess oestrogen**: Klinefelter syndrome, obesity, chronic liver disease, some hormone treatments.
- Previous chest radiotherapy.
- Age.

**Gynaecomastia** (benign breast enlargement in men) is very common and is not cancer, although they can be hard to tell apart without tests.

## Symptoms

- A hard, painless **lump**, usually behind the nipple or areola.
- A **pulled-in nipple**, discharge (sometimes bloody) or a sore.
- Enlarged lymph nodes in the armpit.

## Diagnosis

Examination, **mammography** and **ultrasound**, and **core needle biopsy** with receptor and HER2 testing. **Genetic testing** is recommended for all men with breast cancer, because the result helps treatment and their relatives.

## Treatment

- **Surgery**: usually a **mastectomy**, because the tumour is near the nipple and there is little tissue, with **sentinel node biopsy**.
- **Radiotherapy** depending on size and nodes.
- **Hormone therapy**: **tamoxifen** for 5 to 10 years is the treatment of choice. Aromatase inhibitors alone are less effective in men and, if used, are combined with drugs that suppress testosterone.
- **Chemotherapy** and **anti-HER2 drugs** depending on risk and subtype, as in women.
- In metastatic disease, the same options as in women, including CDK4/6 inhibitors.

Tamoxifen's effects in men (hot flushes, lower sex drive, weight gain) lead to it being stopped more often: discussing them with the team helps find solutions.

## Outlook

Stage for stage, the outlook is similar to women's. Differences in overall survival are mainly due to later diagnosis and older age.

## After treatment

Yearly mammography of the remaining breast, clinical review and, for *BRCA2* carriers, surveillance of the **prostate**, pancreas and skin according to guidelines. Talking about the diagnosis can be harder for men because of stigma; specific support groups exist.
