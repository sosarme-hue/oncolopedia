New cancer drugs

Antibody-drug conjugates, bispecifics, mRNA vaccines, KRAS inhibitors and cell therapies: what the new treatments in the news are and where they stand.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs)

An antibody-drug conjugate is an antibody carrying a very potent chemotherapy payload attached by a chemical linker. It recognises a protein on tumour cells, enters them and releases the drug inside. They are called "Trojan horses". Trastuzumab deruxtecan has changed treatment for breast cancers with low HER2 levels, and enfortumab vedotin for bladder cancer. They have their own side effects, such as eye or lung problems depending on the product.

Bispecific antibodies

They recognise two targets at once: one on the tumour cell and one on the T lymphocyte, which they "bring close" so it can destroy the cell. They are already used in myeloma, lymphomas, lymphoblastic leukaemia and small cell lung cancer (tarlatamab). They can cause cytokine release syndrome, so first doses are given under monitoring.

KRAS inhibitors

KRAS is the most frequently mutated oncogene in cancer and for decades was considered impossible to block. Since 2021 there have been drugs against the G12C variant (sotorasib, adagrasib), useful in some lung and colorectal cancers, and others against more variants are being studied, important for pancreatic cancer.

Therapeutic mRNA vaccines

They are made from the mutations in each patient's tumour to teach the immune system to recognise it. In melanoma and other cancers, combined with immunotherapy after surgery, they have given promising phase II results and are being evaluated in phase III. They are not yet approved for routine use.

Cell therapies

As well as the CAR-T approved for blood cancers, CAR-T for solid tumours, tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy (already approved in some countries for melanoma) and "off-the-shelf" donor cells are being studied.

Radiopharmaceuticals

Molecules that seek out a tumour target carrying a radioactive isotope. Lutetium-177-PSMA in prostate cancer and lutetium-177-dotatate in neuroendocrine tumours are already in use, and many more are in development.

How to know if it is available

A drug may be approved in the United States and not in Europe, or approved but not yet funded by your health system. Check with the FDA, the EMA or your national medicines agency, and ask your oncologist.
