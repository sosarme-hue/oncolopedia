Diagnosis

Imaging tests

Ultrasound, CT, MRI and PET: what each is for in cancer, when they are used and why not every test is right in every case.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

What they are for

Imaging helps to detect a lesion, guide a biopsy, find out how far the cancer has spread (stage), plan surgery or radiotherapy, assess response to treatment and carry out follow-up. Each test answers some questions better than others, so the choice depends on the tumour and the clinical question.

The main tests

Test How it works Common uses
Ultrasound Sound waves, no radiation Breast, thyroid, liver, kidney, testicle, lymph nodes; guiding biopsies
Mammography Low-dose X-rays Breast cancer screening and diagnosis
CT scan X-rays that create cross-sections of the body Staging of chest, abdomen and pelvis; follow-up
MRI Magnetic field, no radiation Brain, spinal cord, liver, rectum, prostate, soft tissue, breast in selected cases
PET-CT A radioactive tracer (often labelled glucose) that builds up in very active areas Lymphomas, lung, melanoma and others; finding metastases; assessing response
Bone scan A tracer that builds up in bone Bone metastases in prostate or breast cancer

Worth knowing

  • Not every test is useful in every cancer. Testing "just in case" can find unimportant findings that lead to more tests and anxiety.
  • An image can suggest cancer, but a biopsy is almost always needed to confirm it.
  • In follow-up, images are read together with symptoms, examination and the treatment received. After radiotherapy or immunotherapy, changes that are not tumour may be seen.
  • Tests using radiation give a dose justified by their benefit. MRI and ultrasound use no radiation.
  • Some tests use contrast: tell the team about allergies, kidney problems, or metal devices and pacemakers (for MRI).

New techniques

PSMA PET detects prostate cancer very sensitively, whole-body MRI is used in myeloma and inherited syndromes, and artificial intelligence is starting to help read mammograms and lung scans, always under radiologists' supervision.
