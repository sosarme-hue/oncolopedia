Living with cancer

Long-term survivors

More and more people live for many years after cancer. What follow-up they need, what late effects can appear and how to look after their health afterwards.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

More and more survivors

Thanks to earlier diagnosis and better treatment, survival has risen steadily. Worldwide there are more than 50 million people alive within five years of a cancer diagnosis, according to IARC estimates, and in the US alone more than 18 million cancer survivors. People are usually called long-term survivors five years after diagnosis without disease, though each cancer has its own timescales.

Follow-up

After treatment, regular check-ups look for possible relapse, monitor late effects and promote health. How often and which tests depend on the cancer; more tests are not always better. Over the years, part of the follow-up may move to the family doctor, coordinated with oncology.

Late effects

Some treatment effects persist or appear years later:

  • Persistent tiredness and memory or concentration problems.
  • Heart: some chemotherapy drugs, antibodies and radiotherapy to the chest increase cardiovascular risk.
  • Bones: osteoporosis from hormone therapy or early menopause.
  • Lymphoedema after lymph node removal.
  • Neuropathy, hearing problems.
  • Fertility, sex life and early menopause.
  • Second cancers, from treatment or from the same predisposition.
  • Fear of recurrence, anxiety or depression.

Survivors of childhood cancer need especially long follow-up.

Looking after your health

  • Do not smoke, limit alcohol, keep a healthy weight, eat well and exercise.
  • Take part in the screening due for your age.
  • Check blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar, because of cardiovascular risk.
  • Ask for a care plan at the end of treatment, with a summary of what you received and the recommended follow-up.

Getting back to life

Many people say that after cancer "nothing is the same". Returning to work, relationships and plans takes time. Patient groups and psychological support help at this stage too.
