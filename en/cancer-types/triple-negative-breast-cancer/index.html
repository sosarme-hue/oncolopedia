Cancer types

Triple-negative breast cancer

A subtype without hormone receptors or HER2, more common in young women and BRCA1 carriers. Chemotherapy and immunotherapy have greatly improved results.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
What it is Breast cancer with no oestrogen or progesterone receptors and no excess HER2
How common About 10-15% of breast cancers
Who More common in women under 40, women of African ancestry and BRCA1 carriers
Treatment Chemotherapy, often before surgery, with immunotherapy for high-risk disease
Feature Relapses, if they happen, are usually within the first three to five years

What it is

It is called triple-negative because the biopsy shows none of the three classic treatment targets: the oestrogen receptor, the progesterone receptor and excess HER2 protein. So it does not respond to hormone therapy or traditional anti-HER2 drugs. It is usually high grade and fast-growing, but also particularly sensitive to chemotherapy.

In fact it groups several types with different biology: basal-like (the majority), lymphocyte-rich tumours with a better outlook, androgen receptor-positive tumours and some indolent special types, such as adenoid cystic carcinoma.

Risk factors

  • Variants in BRCA1: about 70% of breast cancers in BRCA1 carriers are triple-negative, and 10-20% of triple-negative cancers have an inherited variant in BRCA1, BRCA2 or other genes. So genetic testing is recommended for everyone with this subtype, at least up to 60 according to guidelines.
  • Young age and being premenopausal.
  • African ancestry.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Like other breast cancers, it is usually noticed as a lump, sometimes growing quickly between two mammograms (interval cancer). Diagnosis uses mammography, ultrasound and core needle biopsy, where immunohistochemistry confirms the absence of receptors and HER2. Staging is done if the tumour is large or nodes are involved, along with genetic testing.

Treating early disease

  • For small tumours (under 1-2 cm with no nodes), surgery first and then chemotherapy depending on size.
  • For the rest, neoadjuvant chemotherapy (before surgery). The standard regimen for stage II-III combines pembrolizumab (immunotherapy) with carboplatin, taxanes and anthracyclines, based on the KEYNOTE-522 trial, which improved overall survival.
  • Breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy and lymph node assessment.
  • Radiotherapy depending on surgery and stage.
  • If tumour remains after chemotherapy, treatment continues with capecitabine, or olaparib in BRCA carriers, and the year of pembrolizumab is completed.
  • Achieving a pathological complete response (no tumour left in the surgical specimen) is a very good sign.

Metastatic disease

  • If the tumour expresses PD-L1, immunotherapy with chemotherapy first-line.
  • Sacituzumab govitecan, an antibody-drug conjugate against TROP2, in later lines, and trastuzumab deruxtecan if the tumour is HER2-low.
  • PARP inhibitors (olaparib, talazoparib) for BRCA carriers.
  • Single or combined chemotherapy.
  • Clinical trials of new antibody-drug conjugates.

Outlook

The risk of relapse is higher than for other subtypes in the first years, but if there is no relapse within five years it is unlikely later. Survival in early disease has clearly improved with immunotherapy: in KEYNOTE-522, more than 80% of patients with stage II-III disease were alive at five years. Metastatic disease remains hard to treat, although antibody-drug conjugates have extended survival.

  • Is immunotherapy right for me and what effects can it have?
  • When will I get my genetic test result and what does it mean for my family?
