Cancer types

Pancreatic cancer

One of the hardest cancers to treat, because it is usually found late. What is known about its causes, its symptoms and progress in treatment.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common About 510,000 cases worldwide in 2022
Most common type Ductal adenocarcinoma (over 90%). Neuroendocrine tumours are a different disease
Signs Jaundice (yellow skin), upper abdominal or back pain, weight loss, new-onset diabetes
Risks Smoking, obesity, diabetes, chronic pancreatitis, family history
Survival Around 10% at five years; higher if surgery is possible

What it is

The pancreas is a gland behind the stomach that makes digestive juices and hormones such as insulin. Ductal adenocarcinoma starts in the cells of its ducts and is what people usually mean by "pancreatic cancer". Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours, much rarer, behave and are treated very differently.

Why it is found late

The pancreas lies deep in the abdomen and the tumour causes no symptoms until it grows or invades nearby structures. There is no useful screening test for the general population. People with a high inherited risk (for example several affected relatives or variants in BRCA2, PALB2 or CDKN2A) may be offered surveillance in specialist units.

Symptoms

  • Jaundice: yellow skin and eyes, dark urine, pale stools and itching, when the tumour is in the head of the pancreas and blocks the bile duct.
  • Upper abdominal pain spreading to the back.
  • Weight and appetite loss, indigestion, fatty stools.
  • Diabetes appearing suddenly in someone over 50 who is not overweight.

Diagnosis

Pancreas-protocol contrast CT, endoscopic ultrasound with biopsy and the CA 19-9 marker (useful for follow-up, not diagnosis). The crucial question is whether the tumour is resectable, that is, whether it can be removed completely.

Treatment

  • Resectable: surgery (such as pancreaticoduodenectomy, the Whipple procedure) in high-volume centres, followed by chemotherapy. Chemotherapy is often given first.
  • Locally advanced: chemotherapy, sometimes with radiotherapy, to try to make surgery possible.
  • Metastatic: combination chemotherapy (such as FOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine with nab-paclitaxel). Testing the tumour and germline is recommended, because some changes allow specific treatments.
  • Early palliative care for pain, jaundice, nutrition and emotional support.

Research

New drugs against KRAS, the oncogene altered in over 90% of these tumours, personalised mRNA vaccines and early detection in high-risk groups are being studied.

See also: Palliative care, Clinical trials.
