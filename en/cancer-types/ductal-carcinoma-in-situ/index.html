Cancer types

Ductal carcinoma in situ

A breast lesion in which malignant cells have not left the ducts. It is almost always found on a mammogram and has an excellent outlook.

At a glance
What it is Malignant cells inside the breast ducts, without invasion
How common About 20% of cancers found by screening
How it is found Almost always as microcalcifications on a mammogram, without symptoms
Treatment Breast-conserving surgery with or without radiotherapy, or mastectomy; sometimes hormone therapy
Outlook Very low breast cancer mortality, about 3% at 20 years

What it is

In ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) the cells lining the breast ducts look like cancer cells but have not broken through the basement membrane around the duct. So they cannot reach the lymph nodes or spread. It is considered stage 0 breast cancer.

Not all DCIS progresses: a proportion, hard to predict, would turn into invasive cancer over the years if left untreated. The risk is higher for high-grade, extensive DCIS and DCIS with dead cells in the centre of the ducts (comedonecrosis).

Lobular carcinoma in situ is different: it is now seen mainly as a marker of higher cancer risk in both breasts, and is not usually treated with extensive surgery except in particular variants.

How common it is

Before mammography screening DCIS was rare. Today it makes up about one in five breast cancers diagnosed in screening programmes. Its rise is one of the most cited examples of overdiagnosis, because some of these lesions would never have caused harm.

Symptoms and diagnosis

It almost never causes symptoms. Rarely there is a lump, nipple discharge or an eczema-like nipple lesion (Paget's disease).

Diagnosis involves:

  1. Mammography: usually shows clustered, fine or branching microcalcifications.
  2. Vacuum-assisted biopsy guided by mammography (stereotactic), which takes several samples.
  3. Testing of hormone receptors, usually positive.
  4. Sometimes MRI to assess the extent.

If the biopsy shows DCIS, surgery may find areas of invasive cancer, so the diagnosis sometimes changes after the operation.

Treatment

The aim is to prevent invasive cancer developing in the same breast.

  • Breast-conserving surgery (lumpectomy) with clear margins, often after marking the lesion with a wire or seed. A margin of at least 2 mm is recommended.
  • Radiotherapy after breast-conserving surgery: halves recurrences in the breast, although it does not change survival. For small, low-grade lesions with wide margins in older women, leaving it out may be considered.
  • Mastectomy if the DCIS is very extensive or in several areas, with the option of immediate reconstruction.
  • Sentinel node biopsy only with mastectomy or suspected invasion, because it could not be done afterwards.
  • Hormone therapy (tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors for five years) for receptor-positive DCIS treated with breast-conserving surgery: reduces the risk of new lesions in both breasts. Its benefit is weighed against its side effects.

Active monitoring

Several trials (LORIS in the UK, LORD in Europe and COMET in the US) are testing whether low-risk DCIS can be monitored with mammograms instead of surgery. Early COMET results, published in 2024, showed no more invasive cancers at two years with monitoring, but it is not yet standard practice outside trials.

Outlook

After treatment, recurrence in the breast is about 10-15% at ten years with breast-conserving surgery alone, and lower with radiotherapy. Half of recurrences are DCIS again and half invasive cancer. The risk of dying from breast cancer is very low, about 3% at 20 years, somewhat higher in young women and Black women according to large registries.

After treatment

Yearly mammography of both breasts, clinical review and attention to the effects of hormone therapy if taken. Staying active, avoiding alcohol and keeping a healthy weight reduce the risk of new cancers.

Questions for your team

  • Is my DCIS low, intermediate or high grade?
  • Do I need radiotherapy or can I avoid it?
  • Would hormone therapy benefit me?
  • Is there an active monitoring trial I could join?
