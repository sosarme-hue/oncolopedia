## Against the prevailing view

In the 1970s breast cancer was thought too common and complex to have a single genetic cause. Mary-Claire King, who had earlier shown that humans and chimpanzees share 99% of their DNA, argued the opposite: in some families with many cases, especially in young women, there had to be a responsible gene.

## Seventeen years of searching

For 17 years King studied hundreds of families with breast cancer, using statistical methods of **genetic linkage**. In 1990 she announced that the gene lay on the long arm of **chromosome 17**. She called it ***BRCA1***. Her finding set off an international race, and the gene was isolated in 1994. In 1995 ***BRCA2*** was identified.

## From laboratory to clinic

Women with *BRCA1* mutations have a very high risk of breast and ovarian cancer. Today there is **genetic counselling**, testing, surveillance programmes, preventive surgery and drugs (**PARP inhibitors**) specific to these tumours. King has argued that all women, not only those from families with many cases, should be able to know their risk.

## Genetics and human rights

From 1984 she applied genetics to identifying the **stolen children** of the Argentine dictatorship, helping the Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo reunite them with their families.
