## When the goal changes

There comes a time in some cancers when treatments can no longer control the illness, or their burden outweighs their benefit. The goal then becomes **living as well as possible** the time that remains: controlling symptoms, being where you want to be and with whom you want. That is the work of **palliative care**, which can also be offered much earlier, alongside cancer treatment. See [Palliative care](../../treatments/palliative-care/).

## Common worries

- **Pain and suffering**: almost always they can be relieved. In the final days, if symptoms cannot be controlled otherwise, **palliative sedation** is possible.
- **Being a burden** to the family.
- **Where to die**: at home, in hospital, in a hospice. Home palliative care teams make it possible to stay at home.
- Leaving things unresolved: practical, family or emotional.
- What will happen to children and other dependants.

## Preparing what matters

- **Talking with your team** about what matters to you and what you would not want (for example, resuscitation or admission to intensive care).
- **Advance directives** (in Spain, *instrucciones previas*): a document in which you set out your wishes about care and name a representative. In other countries they are called a *living will* or advance decision.
- Practical matters: a will, documents, passwords, the care of dependants.
- Saying what you want to say: thanks, forgiveness, farewells. Some people write letters or record videos for their loved ones. **Dignity therapy**, a brief intervention in which the person tells what they want to leave to others, has been shown to help some patients.
- In Spain, **euthanasia** has been regulated since 2021 under strict requirements; ask your team if you want information.

## Supporting the family

- Keep them informed, according to what the patient wants to share.
- Make room for them to rest and to take turns.
- Prepare children for what will happen, with simple language. See [Talking to children about cancer](../talking-to-children-about-cancer/).
- Palliative care teams also support the family.

## Grief

Grief is the normal response to loss. It is not a straight line: sadness, anger, guilt, relief and even moments of calm alternate. It often begins before the death (anticipatory grief). Most people adapt over months, with the support of those around them.

Ask for help if, after a long time, the pain prevents you from carrying on with your life, if you cannot accept the death or if you have thoughts of not wanting to live. Many palliative care services, psycho-oncology units and associations offer **grief support**. See also [Carers and family organisation](../../living-with-cancer/carers-and-family-organisation/).
