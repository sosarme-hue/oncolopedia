## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | Found in about 1% of appendicectomies |
| **Types** | Neuroendocrine tumours, mucinous neoplasms (LAMN, HAMN), adenocarcinomas |
| **Pseudomyxoma** | Mucus building up in the peritoneum from a ruptured mucinous neoplasm |
| **Treatment** | Appendicectomy or right hemicolectomy; cytoreductive surgery with HIPEC for pseudomyxoma |

## Types

- **Appendiceal neuroendocrine tumours**: the most common, especially in young people. Under 2 cm, appendicectomy usually cures them.
- **Low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm (LAMN)** and high-grade (HAMN): produce mucus and can rupture.
- **Adenocarcinoma** (mucinous, signet ring cell or colonic type): more aggressive; treated like colon cancer, with right hemicolectomy and chemotherapy.
- **Goblet cell adenocarcinoma** (formerly "goblet cell carcinoid").

## Pseudomyxoma peritonei

When a mucinous appendix tumour ruptures, its cells seed the peritoneum and produce **gelatinous mucus** that slowly builds up ("jelly belly"). It causes a growing waist, hernias, early fullness and, in women, is often found as an ovarian mass.

The reference treatment is **complete cytoreductive surgery with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC)**, developed by Paul Sugarbaker. In specialist centres, more than 70% of patients with low-grade pseudomyxoma survive 10 years. The UK has two national centres (Basingstoke and The Christie, Manchester).

## Diagnosis

CT and MRI, CEA, CA 19-9 and CA 125 markers, colonoscopy, and review by an expert pathologist. Laparoscopy assesses extent (peritoneal cancer index).
