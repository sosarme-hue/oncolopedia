## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **How common** | The most frequently diagnosed cancer in women worldwide: about 2.3 million cases in 2022 |
| **Who** | Mostly women over 50. About 1% of cases occur in men |
| **Typical sign** | A new lump in the breast or armpit, changes in the skin or nipple |
| **Screening** | Mammography every two or three years, depending on the country, usually from around age 50 |
| **Survival** | Around 85-90% at five years in Western Europe, with large differences by stage |

## What it is

Breast cancer starts in the cells of the ducts (ductal carcinoma) or lobules (lobular carcinoma) of the breast. When malignant cells have not left their place of origin it is called **carcinoma in situ**; when they break through, **invasive carcinoma**. The most common type is invasive carcinoma "of no special type", formerly called ductal.

Rather than one cancer, it is several diseases. That is why the pathology report looks at three markers that change treatment:

- **Hormone receptors** (oestrogen and progesterone): present in about two in three tumours. They indicate that hormone therapy will work.
- **HER2**: a protein that, in excess, drives tumour growth. There are drugs aimed specifically at it.
- **Triple negative**: no hormone receptors and no HER2. More common in younger women and in carriers of *BRCA1* variants.

## Symptoms

- A lump or thickened area in the breast or armpit, often painless.
- A change in the shape or size of the breast.
- Skin that is pulled in, red or dimpled like orange peel.
- A nipple that has recently turned inwards, or nipple discharge, especially if bloody.

Most breast lumps are not cancer, but any change that persists should be checked.

## Risk factors

Age and sex are the main ones. Risk is also raised by family history, inherited variants in *BRCA1*, *BRCA2* and other genes, early periods or late menopause, having no children or having them later, alcohol, obesity after menopause, inactivity and some hormone treatments. Breastfeeding and physical activity lower it.

## Diagnosis

A symptom leads to a **mammogram**, often with an **ultrasound**, and if there is a suspicious area, a **core needle biopsy**. Staging tests depend on tumour size and lymph nodes. Some people are offered **genetic counselling** because of their personal or family history.

## Treatment

Treatment is agreed by a team of surgeons, medical oncologists and radiation oncologists, according to stage and tumour biology:

- **Surgery**: breast-conserving surgery (lumpectomy) followed by radiotherapy, or mastectomy, with the option of reconstruction. **Sentinel lymph node** biopsy often avoids removing all the armpit nodes.
- **Hormone therapy** (tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors) for 5-10 years in hormone-sensitive tumours.
- **Chemotherapy**, before (neoadjuvant) or after (adjuvant) surgery, depending on risk. Genomic tests on the tumour help identify who can safely skip it.
- **Anti-HER2 therapies** such as trastuzumab and pertuzumab, and antibody-drug conjugates.
- **Immunotherapy** in some triple-negative tumours, and other targeted drugs such as CDK4/6 or PARP inhibitors.

## Outlook

It is one of the cancers with the best survival when found at a localised stage. Stage, lymph nodes, grade and biological subtype are the most important factors. Hormone-sensitive tumours can come back many years later, so follow-up is long.
