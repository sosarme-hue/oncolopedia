FAQ

Do alternative therapies work?

The difference between complementary therapies, which can help people feel better, and alternative therapies, which replace treatment and put lives at risk.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

Complementary is not the same as alternative

  • A complementary therapy is used alongside medical treatment to ease symptoms or improve wellbeing: exercise, yoga, meditation, massage, acupuncture for some nausea, or music therapy. Some have evidence of benefit for quality of life, and hospitals include them in what is called integrative oncology.
  • An alternative therapy is used instead of medical treatment, with the promise of curing cancer. None has proven it.

What the data say

A study of more than 800 patients with breast, prostate, lung and bowel cancer showed that those who chose alternative medicine alone had a 2.5 times higher risk of death than those who received conventional treatment. Another study found that patients using complementary medicine were more likely to refuse some conventional treatment, and that explained their poorer survival.

Watch out for interactions

Some herbs and supplements interfere with treatment. St John's wort reduces the effect of many drugs; grapefruit alters their metabolism; high-dose antioxidants might reduce the effectiveness of radiotherapy and some chemotherapy; other products damage the liver. Always tell your team about anything you take, even if it is "natural".

Warning signs

Promises of cure, testimonials instead of studies, high prices, attacks on conventional medicine and requests to stop treatment. If in doubt, ask your oncologist or check reliable sources.
