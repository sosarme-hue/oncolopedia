William Halsted

The American surgeon who created the radical mastectomy and the modern school of surgery. His operation dominated breast cancer treatment for 80 years.

The Johns Hopkins surgeon

William Stewart Halsted was the first chief of surgery at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore. He introduced rubber gloves to the operating theatre, a meticulous surgical technique and the residency system for training surgeons that is still used worldwide.

The radical mastectomy

At the time, operated breast cancer almost always came back. Halsted thought cancer grew in an orderly way, from the tumour to the armpit nodes and from there to the rest of the body, and that very wide surgery could cut it out at the root. His radical mastectomy removed the breast, both pectoral muscles and all the armpit nodes. In 1894 he reported a dramatic fall in local recurrences.

Eighty years of dogma

The operation became standard treatment and grew even more extensive in some "super-radical" variants. It left serious after-effects: a swollen arm (lymphoedema), pain and loss of strength. From the 1970s, Bernard Fisher's trials showed that breast cancer can be a systemic disease from the start and that smaller operations with radiotherapy gave the same survival.

A life with two sides

Halsted became addicted to cocaine after experimenting with it as a local anaesthetic, and later to morphine; he kept his addiction secret for decades.
