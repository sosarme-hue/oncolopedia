Gertrude Elion

The American chemist who designed drugs rationally, including 6-mercaptopurine for childhood leukaemia. A Nobel laureate without a doctorate.

Cancer in the family

Gertrude Belle Elion decided to study chemistry at 15, when she watched her grandfather die of stomach cancer. After graduating, several companies turned her down because she was a woman. She worked as a teacher and in labs without pay, and began a doctorate that she had to abandon to keep her job.

Rational drug design

In 1944 she joined Burroughs Wellcome with George Hitchings. Instead of testing substances at random, they studied how nucleic acids are made and designed molecules resembling purines to trick fast-dividing cells. This gave rise in 1950 to 6-mercaptopurine, which produced remissions in childhood acute lymphoblastic leukaemia and is still part of its maintenance treatment.

Much more than one drug

The same method produced thioguanine (leukaemias), azathioprine (which made kidney transplants possible), allopurinol (gout), aciclovir (the first effective antiviral against herpes) and later work leading to zidovudine against HIV.

A Nobel without a doctorate

In 1988 she received the Nobel Prize in Medicine with Hitchings and James Black. She never earned a research doctorate, although she received dozens of honorary ones.
