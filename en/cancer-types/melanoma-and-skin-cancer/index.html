Cancer types

Melanoma

The most serious skin cancer. Found very early, surgery cures the great majority. Immunotherapy has lengthened survival in advanced disease, although not every patient responds.

General information only. It does not replace advice from your care team and should not be used to choose a treatment.

At a glance
How common 330,000 cases a year worldwide, and rising; about 17,500 in the UK
Who Any adult age; one of the most common cancers between 25 and 50
Main cause Ultraviolet radiation (sun and sunbeds), especially childhood sunburn
Sign A new or changing mole (ABCDE rule)
Treatment Surgery with sentinel node; immunotherapy and targeted therapy (BRAF/MEK)
Survival Over 90% at five years; over 98% for thin melanomas

What it is

Melanoma arises from melanocytes, the cells that make skin pigment. It can develop in an existing mole (25-30%) or, more often, as a new spot. Although much less common than basal cell and squamous cell carcinomas, it causes most skin cancer deaths because it can spread.

Types

Type Features
Superficial spreading The most common (60-70%); grows outwards before growing deeper
Nodular Grows deep quickly; often does not follow the ABCDE rule (EFG rule: elevated, firm, growing)
Lentigo maligna On the face of older people with a lot of accumulated sun
Acral lentiginous On palms, soles and nails; the most common in people with dark skin and Asian people; not sun-related
Mucosal In mouth, nose, genitals, anus
Uveal In the eye; has its own sheet

Risk factors

  • Sunburn, especially in childhood and adolescence, and intense intermittent exposure.
  • Sunbeds: raise risk by 75% if used before 35 (IARC group 1 carcinogen).
  • Fair skin, blond or red hair, light eyes, freckles, burning easily.
  • Many moles (over 50) or atypical moles.
  • Family history (CDKN2A, CDK4, BAP1, MITF mutations).
  • Previous melanoma, weakened immunity.

Prevention

  • Avoid the sun between 11 am and 3 pm, seek shade.
  • Clothing, wide-brimmed hat and sunglasses.
  • Broad-spectrum sunscreen SPF 30 or higher, reapplied every 2 hours and after swimming. The Nambour trial (Australia) showed daily use halves invasive melanomas.
  • No sunbeds (banned for under-18s in the UK).
  • Protect children especially.
  • Monthly self-examination of all the skin, with a mirror or another person, and dermatology checks for high-risk people (sometimes with digital dermoscopy and total body mapping).

Symptoms: the ABCDE rule

Letter What to look for
Asymmetry One half does not match the other
Border Irregular, jagged or blurred
Colour Several colours (brown, black, red, white, blue)
Diameter Over 6 mm (although it can be smaller)
Evolution Changes in size, shape or colour, itches or bleeds

Also the "ugly duckling" sign: a mole that looks different from all the person's others. And in nails, a new or widening dark band.

Diagnosis

  1. Dermoscopy: a lit magnifier revealing structures invisible to the naked eye.
  2. Complete removal (excision biopsy) of the suspicious lesion.
  3. The report measures Breslow thickness (in millimetres), ulceration and mitoses: the key prognostic factors.
  4. Sentinel node biopsy for melanomas over 0.8 mm or ulcerated.
  5. In higher stages: CT or PET-CT, brain MRI and BRAF mutation testing (present in 40-50%).

Stages

Stage Description Approx. 5-year survival
0 In situ, only in the epidermis ~100%
I Thin (≤ 1-2 mm), no nodes 95-99%
II Thicker or ulcerated, no nodes 80-95%
III Nodes or in-transit metastases 40-90% by substage
IV Distant metastases Over 50% with combined immunotherapy in some groups

Treatment

  • Wider excision margins (0.5-2 cm depending on thickness).
  • Sentinel node; if positive, full node dissection is no longer usual (MSLT-II and DeCOG-SLT trials), but ultrasound surveillance and systemic treatment.
  • Adjuvant treatment (after surgery) for stages IIB-IV: immunotherapy (nivolumab or pembrolizumab) for a year, or dabrafenib with trametinib for BRAF mutations.
  • Neoadjuvant treatment: in melanoma with palpable nodes, a few doses of pembrolizumab before surgery improve results (SWOG S1801 and NADINA trials, the latter with nivolumab and ipilimumab).
  • Advanced disease:
  • Nivolumab with ipilimumab: half of patients are alive at 10 years (CheckMate 067 trial), unimaginable before 2011.
  • Nivolumab with relatlimab (anti-LAG-3), or anti-PD-1 alone.
  • BRAF V600: BRAF and MEK inhibitors (dabrafenib + trametinib, encorafenib + binimetinib, vemurafenib + cobimetinib).
  • Tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy, lifileucel, approved in the USA in 2024.
  • Radiosurgery for brain metastases, which also respond to immunotherapy.

Outlook

Immunotherapy has transformed metastatic melanoma: before 2011 median survival was 6-9 months; today, with nivolumab and ipilimumab, the median exceeds 6 years.

After treatment

Skin and lymph node checks for at least 5-10 years, self-examination, strict sun protection and monitoring of immunotherapy side effects (thyroid, adrenal, pituitary, colitis, hepatitis). First-degree relatives should have their skin checked.

What's new

  • Personalised mRNA vaccines (mRNA-4157/V940) with pembrolizumab after surgery: fewer relapses in KEYNOTE-942; now in phase 3.
  • Artificial intelligence to analyse dermoscopic images.
  • Bispecific antibodies and new cell therapies.

Questions for your team

  • What is the Breslow thickness of my melanoma? Is it ulcerated?
  • Do I need a sentinel node biopsy?
  • Does it have a BRAF mutation?
  • Would I benefit from treatment after surgery?
  • How often should my skin be checked?
