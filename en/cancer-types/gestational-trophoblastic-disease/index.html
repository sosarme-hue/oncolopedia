Cancer types

Gestational trophoblastic disease

Tumours arising from placental tissue, from hydatidiform mole to choriocarcinoma. They are among the most curable cancers, even with metastases.

At a glance
What it is Abnormal growth of the tissue that forms the placenta
How common Mole: 1 in 1,000 pregnancies in Europe; more in Asia
Marker The pregnancy hormone (hCG), which allows follow-up with blood tests
Treatment Suction evacuation; chemotherapy (methotrexate or combinations) if it persists
Cure Close to 100% for low risk and over 90% for high risk

Types

  • Complete and partial hydatidiform mole: an abnormal pregnancy with no viable fetus (complete) or an abnormal fetus (partial). They are premalignant.
  • Invasive mole: the mole grows into the wall of the womb.
  • Choriocarcinoma: a malignant tumour that can follow any pregnancy, even a normal one or a miscarriage, and spread to lung, brain or liver.
  • Placental site trophoblastic tumour and epithelioid trophoblastic tumour: rare, produce little hCG and respond less to chemotherapy, so they are treated with surgery.

Persistent or malignant forms are grouped as gestational trophoblastic neoplasia.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Vaginal bleeding in the first trimester, a womb larger than expected, severe sickness, overactive thyroid or early pre-eclampsia. Ultrasound shows a typical appearance and hCG is very high. After any pregnancy, persistent bleeding should prompt an hCG test.

Treatment and follow-up

  1. Evacuation of the mole by suction.
  2. hCG follow-up weekly until normal and then for some months. Most are cured with nothing more.
  3. If hCG plateaus or rises (15-20% of complete moles), the WHO/FIGO prognostic score is calculated: - Low risk: single-agent methotrexate or actinomycin D. - High risk: combination chemotherapy (EMA-CO); immunotherapy in resistant cases.
  4. Avoid a new pregnancy during follow-up, using effective contraception.

Outlook

It is one of the most curable tumours in oncology: almost all low-risk patients are cured and most keep their fertility, with normal later pregnancies. Follow-up in reference centres, such as the UK's national centres, is recommended.
